The boy brought him the coin on a wet Deepcold evening, holding it the way Hollows children held anything that might be a trap — flat on the open palm, arm out, ready to drop it and run. Korbin Tare knew what it was before he picked it up. He had seen its brothers twice in three years. He had hoped hard not to see a third.

"Man on the Level Three stair," the boy said. Pell, fourteen, Stillblade runner since the autumn, quick and bird-boned and proud of his colours. "Said there's more where it come from. Said a lad who knows the old waterways could earn a stack of them, just for walking ahead of some freight with a lamp. Said—" Pell screwed his face up, getting it exact, because Korbin paid for exact — "*no knives, no questions, no names.* Then he went down. Not up."

The coin was a Crown, full weight, clean as a chapel. That was the wrongness. Hollows coin was biography: clipped, sweated, knife-marked, passed through a hundred fists. This had come from a mint to a purse to a palm. New money, spent below Level Three, where new money never came except to buy something the surface didn't sell. The first time Korbin had seen its like was 1144, in the hand of a Stillblade fence the Pact had tried to turn. The second was a year and a half gone. Same minting. Same weightless courtesy. Both times he had shut it down hard and buried the contact, and both times he had lain awake after, doing the arithmetic that came with his chair: the Shadow Pact had tried twice through Stillblade channels. Twice that he had caught.

"You said?"

"Said I'd think on it." Pell's chin came up. "That's right, isn't it? You always say — don't say no to a wolf in a tunnel. Say maybe, and walk backward."

"That's right." Korbin turned the Crown over. The second wrongness had come that same morning, from the toll-ledger the Grey Hands thought nobody else could read. The Hands had taken money this month — good money, over the odds — *not* to move freight through the old river-tunnel. Purchased absence. A gang paid to stay out of its own smuggling-run. Somebody wanted the old waterway empty, dark, and unwatched, for something that walked with its own lamp-boys ahead of it.

Down, not up. Toward the deep levels, where the maps stopped and the flood began. Korbin put the Crown in his pocket, against policy, because it was evidence now and evidence belonged in the ledger.

"Pell. If the man asks again — you come to me first. Before you answer. Swear it on your colours."

The boy swore it, easy, pleased to matter. That was the moment Korbin got wrong, he would decide later. Not a mistake of action; a mistake of accounting. He had entered the boy as a scout when he should have entered him as a stake.

The fence was called Harrow, kept a coin-and-curio stall on the Second Terrace, and had survived thirty years between the gangs and the law by perfecting the art of remembering nothing. Inquisitor Maela Vethryn let him perform his forgetting for a quarter-hour before she asked her first real question.

"The man who sold you the bone flute," she said, soft, southern-slow, "the one you can't recall — when he whistled to show you its tone, did he whistle a hymn?"

Harrow's face did what faces did at her questions: stopped acting, briefly, to think. "…A carol, like. Old-fashioned."

"Mm. Did it run down at the end — the last three notes falling, like steps?" She sang them, quietly, in the dusty stall: three descending notes. The cadence of the Whispering Vigil, the Deepcold liturgy of the longest night, which no soul in Everpeak whistled eight weeks out of season — unless the season in their head ran on a different calendar. Unless the night they kept vigil for was their own. "Like that?"

"…Like that exactly. Here, how'd you—"

"And he was buying, not selling, despite the flute. He asked you what moved through the deep markets. Old stone. Black stone. Marked pieces." She watched the confirmation arrive on the fence's face before his caution could close over it, and catalogued: third report in five weeks. A courier-chain assembling itself in her city — hymn-tells and clean coin, interested in the deep levels — and her own order reading every sign as recruitment, the familiar pattern, the comfortable one, because recruitment they knew how to watch.

The refusal came that same afternoon, from the Veil's own chancery, in the gentle language she could have drafted herself. Her request for warranted access below Level Three was returned without grant. The Deep Hollows lay outside the Cathedral's writ and inside the Serpents' peace. An armed Veil descent would be read as war by the Mid-Hollows and produce nothing but bodies. The Inquisitor's energies were better directed at the Pact's surface approaches. Her diligence, as always, was commended.

Maela filed the refusal with her other refusals, of which she kept a drawer. Then she sat in her cell-office with the lamp and the case-wall — six years of string and paper, the long hunt for the Veilkeeper called Sileth, who was a name and a hand and never yet a face — and let herself think the thought her superiors would not fund. Recruitment is hiring. This is logistics. You did not buy fences' eyes and gang absences and lamp-boys for persuasion. You bought them to move something. Things that moved had bearings. Bearings could be taken. And there was exactly one instrument in Everpeak that could take a bearing below Level Three without starting a war.

She had met him three times. The meetings had not been conducted as enemies. Each of them, she was quite sure, suspected the other of being useful; and the bleak patient arithmetic of her trade said it was time one of them proved it.

Before he would spend Stillblade legs on it, Korbin went down to the Tunnel Court and bought a bearing from Mother Gren, because in the Hollows you did not move through the deep dark on your own information if older information could be had.

The Court sat where it had sat for sixty years, in the vaulted junction off Level Four where five tunnels met: Gren in her chair of cushions and stolen velvet, blind these twenty years and the better informed for it, her hearers on the steps below her, the lamps green with copper-salt because green light was the Court's flag of truce. Korbin laid the clean Crown on the cloth before her — evidence spent as toll; the ledger would record it — and put his question the Court's way, as a story wanting an ending. Freight moving on the old riverway. The Hands paid to be elsewhere. Lamp-boys hired by wolves. *Where does the old water go, Mother, below where my maps stop?*

The old woman turned the Crown in her fingers, reading the mint of it with her thumb, and her face folded.

"Twice before this winter, that coin's cousin has crossed my cloth," she said. "I'll tell you what I told the coin. The old river ran before the Engineers turned it, and dead water keeps its course. Below the fifth level the old way splits: the wet throat, that drowns; and the dry shelf, that the river cut and left when it was young — a ledge above its own grave, that a man who knows can walk with his boots dry the whole way down." Her thumb stopped on the coin. "The Hands never found the shelf. Their fathers never found it. Somebody's freight is travelling dry, captain. Which means somebody bought a map older than the Hands. And there's no one living sells that map."

"Where does the shelf come out?"

"Where the old river went." Gren leaned back into her cushions. "Into the dark under everything, where the snakes stand guard on a door that doesn't open, and have stood my whole long life, and have never once said why. You know the place I mean. Everyone knows. Nobody knows." She pushed the Crown back across the cloth toward him — refused toll, the Court's gravest commentary. "Keep your money, Tare. I'll not take wages off this one. The Court's bearing, free, and the Court's advice with it: whatever walks the dry shelf this winter wants that quarter of the dark. And the boy you're minded to use for a guide — oh, I hear everything, captain, sit down — is fourteen. The Bone-and-Brass are pushing on your eastern stairs precisely because someone is paying them to keep your eyes east. You are being scheduled. Go down if you must. But go knowing you were invited."

<Break />

They met in the Bell-and-Candle, a tavern on the Level One margin that belonged to neither world and served both, in the back room the keeper kept for trades that needed four walls and no memory. The fourth meeting. Both of them counted. Neither said so.

"You asked for the table this time," Korbin said. "That's new."

"Something is moving through your tunnels toward the Deep Hollows," Maela said, soft, without preamble, because her trade with him had always run on the same coin: specificity, paid up front. "It is being moved for the Shadow Pact, by hirelings engaged through a channel inside the Black Serpents — a channel the Serpent leadership does not know it has. I know the buyer, the hymn its couriers whistle, and what manner of thing it hunts in the deep markets: old marked stone. I do not know the route, the cargo's lodging, or the bearing. You do, or can."

Korbin looked at her for a while. Hollows rules said never confirm. The ledger in his head said something else, and the something else had Pell's face on it.

"And if I do?"

"Then I want the look. One descent. My eyes on where it rests — no warrant, no Blades, no Veil colours below the Third. I come as a woman in a brown coat, under your guiding and your rules. I draw no iron on Serpent ground. I take nothing out but what my memory carries."

"And the price," she said. "Name it." She watched the gang-captain do what he always did at the hinge of a deal, the thing she had put in her case-notes after their first meeting six years ago: go still, and price it like a funeral, not a robbery.

"Three things," Korbin said. "One: the Stillblade was never below the Fourth. Whatever you write, whoever you answer to — we are not in it. Not a thread-colour, not a cant-word. Two: the Pact tried to buy one of mine. A boy. When this is done, your people lose his description. Permanently. Three." The still went deeper. "If it goes wrong down there, Inquisitor, your Cathedral buries its own and forgets the rest. Mine have no Cathedral. If it costs me people, you carry no tale that makes their families pay twice."

"Agreed, all three," Maela said, and meant it, and noted — bleakly, professionally — that she had just contracted to falsify the record for a criminal gang, and that it had cost her conscience nothing at all, and that this fact belonged in a treatise she had not yet dared to write. "And my side's warning, captain. Free, as your Mother Gren gives bearings. I have hunted these people six years. They do not guard their works with sentries. They guard them with arrangements — and the arrangement always spends the local help first. Whatever they paid for passage down there, they have also paid for something to happen behind them. Choose who walks with us as if you were choosing who you can afford."

She watched the sentence land on him like a debt he had already incurred.

"Two days," Korbin said. "Brown coat. Soft boots. If you whistle anything at all down there, Inquisitor, make it in season."

They went down on the second night, four of them: Korbin; the Inquisitor anonymous in brown; Dann, twenty years a Stillblade, the steadiest dark-hand he had; and Pell. There was no honest way around the boy. He had walked point for the freight. He knew the lamp-marks. A guide who had been invited once already was the only key they had that fit. Korbin put him in the middle of the file and made the order of march a wall around him, and knew, doing it, that walls in tunnels face the wrong way as often as not.

The old river-course received them below the Fifth: smooth water-cut stone, black, flowing-shaped. The Engineers' dead gift of 1075. A whole river's architecture standing empty, seventy years drained, the air still and mineral and old. Pell found the shelf where the freight-walk had used it — a ledge half a span proud of the flood, dry and true above the black water that filled its elder grave. They walked it single-file by shuttered lamp. An hour. Two. Past the Hands' painted limit-marks. Past marks older than the Hands. The flood stood glass-still on their left and breathed cold at them. Pell named the lamp-stations in a whisper as they passed: *here we rested; here the freight-men changed poles; here the quiet man stood and counted us by.* Fourteen years old, surefooted, proud, leading his captain through the dark exactly as well as the Pact's silver had taught him to.

That was the thing Korbin chewed on, all the long way down, colder than the flood-breath. Every step of their descent ran on the enemy's training. The Pact had hired the boy to learn the road, and now the road's knowledge walked them in. Invited, Gren had said. The freight had passed five nights since. The dry shelf showed its traffic to a tunnel eye — pole-butts, wax-spots, a crate-corner's scrape — and all of it one-way. Delivered, not retrieved. Whatever had come down here had come to stay.

Near the third hour the shelf bent round a fall of ancient rubble, and Dann, at point, stopped dead and shuttered his lamp to a thread. Around the bend, far off down a gallery no Stillblade map had ever held, there was light. Not lamp-light. Steady, pale, unmoving — the banked glow of a guarded place burning lights it did not fear to show, down where no honest fear would ever come looking.

"That's it, then," Pell breathed, thrilled, terrified, fourteen. "That's where the river went."

"Quiet now," Korbin said, gently. And the four of them went forward into the part of the world that did not appear in anyone's books, to take the look they had each, in their different coin, already paid for.

The cavern opened with no warning the surface ever gives: one moment the gallery's water-cut throat, the next a darkness with size in it. Maela Vethryn stood at the rubble-line where the old river had once fallen roaring into the deep, and looked across at the thing her whole order knew of in fragments and footnotes and had never seen.

The cavern was natural, vast, its far reaches gone in the dark. The light came from cressets — plain iron, plain oil, trimmed by unhurried hands — burning before the rear wall. And the rear wall, where the light reached it, was not wall. It was door. Black stone, seamless, taller than three men, set flush into the living rock as if the rock had healed around it. No handle. No hinge. No join her eye could find. A shape that did not so much stand in the cavern as conclude it. And every span of stone around and above it, wherever the cresset-light climbed, was carved: dense ruled lines of script flowing across the cavern wall, thousands upon thousands of characters. A drowned library, cut in stone.

Wenlyrian. She knew its look the way she knew old blood: from evidence. She could read not one word. And she stood there cataloguing, with the bleak honesty that was her one unbroken vow, the fact that no one she could ever bring here could read it either. The tongue was twelve centuries dead. Its living readers numbered a dozen, and no power in Everpeak could produce one in this cavern without producing also the end of the world's present arrangement of secrets.

The cache was nearer: a dry alcove off the old riverfall, fitted with shelving of new timber — new, plain joinery, weeks old — and on the shelves, crated and corded, the freight. She counted eleven crates, small, uniform. The nearest stood open, its packing turned back. In it, on a bed of wool, lay a hand-sized weight of black stone, dressed and bordered, three Wenlyrian characters cut on its face. A paperweight from a drowned civilization. Inert, minor, meaningless — and provenanced, and door-adjacent, and one of eleven. The whole picture assembled itself in the cresset-light with the quiet click of six years of string pulling taut.

Not an assault. A rehearsal. Small commissions, paid passage, purchased absences; a route proven, a cache established, a supply-line walked end to end by hirelings who knew nothing. The Pact was not at the door. The Pact was building the road to the door, and testing every span of it with freight that could be lost without loss. Her amulet hung silent on her wrist: no Bound Warlock within its reach. No sorcery. Ordinary men, ordinary oil, ordinary timber. That was the part her report could never make her superiors feel. The Pact's patience had no smell of brimstone on it at all. It smelled of carpentry.

And then there were the guards.

Serpent guards — five, then a sixth in the high dark, posted with a discipline no gang lavished on contraband. It took her a long moment at the rubble-line to see what was wrong with their posting, and the seeing reordered the entire case-wall in her head. They were not guarding the cache. They stood between the cache and the door. Every man of them faced outward, away from the black stone, ringing it, backs to the one thing in the cavern worth a fortune to know. The cache sat unwatched in its alcove like delivered firewood. The door was being guarded from the cavern — from the freight, from whatever came down the dead river next. The posting of men who keep a thing shut, not men who keep a thing theirs. Years, the files said. The Fang had posted this watch years ago and never explained; and the Veil had filed it under criminal mystery, monopoly, profit. Standing in the dark at the rubble-line, Maela Vethryn looked at six gang-soldiers holding the world's strangest vigil at the bottom of the world, and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than the cavern did:

Of every power in this city — crown, Cathedral, Council, all of us in our daylight — the only one that has been standing between the Pact and that door, all these years, is them.

She had her look. She turned to give Korbin the withdraw-sign.

The dark behind them was already moving.

<Break />

What it was, in the end, was exactly what the Inquisitor had said it would be. Not sentries. An arrangement.

The Pact's road-builders had left their road trapped — not with iron or sorcery but with men: a rear-guard of four, hirelings of the hirelings, paid to lie up in the dark of the dry shelf's last bend and spend themselves on anything that came down the route behind the freight. Korbin reconstructed it afterward, the way he reconstructed every loss, hour by hour against the ledger of his own decisions. They had been marked at the second lamp-station. Paced. Let pass — let in. And taken on the way out, at the bend above the flooded gallery, in the dark, by men whose only commission was that no witness walk back up the dead river.

The fight was short. Everything is short down there. Dann's lamp. The rush. Black water. The Inquisitor's writing-set held a blade after all; she had sworn not to draw on Serpent ground; this was no one's ground, and she drew. And Pell, fourteen, bird-boned, walking point because he knew the lamp-marks, did in the dark precisely what his captain had drilled into him on the stairs of Level Three — *when it comes, drop the lamp and go flat.* He dropped the lamp. He went flat. The man who had been paid for absences stumbled over him in the blackness and killed him there on the dry shelf, quickly, without malice, by purchased reflex. Then the dark again, and the water lapping.

They brought him up. That was never in question; the Stillblade brought its people up; that was the whole of the code that mattered, the rest was fencing and tolls. Korbin carried the boy the last hour himself, up the dead river's throat with the cold coming off the flood. Dann walked behind with his knife out and wept without sound. The Inquisitor walked last, saying nothing, carrying — Korbin understood it even then, even in the worst hour, because his mind kept its ledger whether he willed it or not — the only other thing that had made it out intact: the look. The cache. The crates. The guards facing outward. The direction of everything. Two people in the climbing dark, holding between them a truth with no place in the world to set it down.

At the Fourth Level margin, where the green light of the Tunnel Court glowed far off down the junction, she stopped him.

"Captain. The terms hold — you were never below. But hear the rest of it once, from me, plainly, because you bought it with him." She said it slow and southern and quiet, and it was the closest thing to a liturgy the boy would get. "What killed him was not a gang and was not a guard. It is a thing that is building a road under your city to a door it should fear and does not. I am going to spend what remains of my service making someone above believe that. I will fail for years. When I stop failing, it will matter that a Stillblade boy walked the road first, and that a Stillblade captain brought back its map." She looked down at Pell's still face, and Korbin watched the patient, methodical, bleak machinery of her pause once, entirely, before it resumed. "Write him in your ledger, Tare. I'll write him in mine. Between the two books, he's carried."

<Break />

The percentage was paid on the seventh day, by the custom: Korbin himself, at the family's door in the Upper Hollows, with the sum in honest mixed coin — no clean Crowns; he had melted the evidence-piece into the general fund rather than let its minting touch the boy's mother's hand. The Stillblade ledger took the entry it took for every member dead on Stillblade work, the entry that was the whole reason men followed him: name, date, *fell below the Fifth, on the gang's business, guiding*; the percentage continuing, quarter on quarter, while the chair was his. The mother asked what her son had been guiding.

"Me," Korbin said, which was the truth, and bore it.

The Bone-and-Brass pressure on the eastern stairs eased that same week, its purpose served, its paymasters gone quiet. The Grey Hands' toll-ledger showed no new absences. The deep markets returned to their ordinary dishonesty. The Hollows, which knew everything and said nothing, added the night to its long unwritten archive; and the Stillblade stood its watches; and their captain walked his levels with the jaw-scar and the patched coat and the Hammergrip knife, and woke before the bells most mornings from a dream of green light and black water, and lay in the dark doing the arithmetic that came with the chair. Twice I caught. Once I watched. What is the count of the times I missed?

The report took Maela Vethryn eleven nights to write, and it was the truest document of her career, which was why she wrote four drafts of everything around the one paragraph she did not soften.

She gave her order what her terms permitted: the courier-chain, the hymn-tell, the purchased absences; the route's existence in the under-levels, source protected; the cache, eleven crates, the rehearsal-reading, the road-not-assault; and the recommendation — she could hear the chancery's gentle refusal already, and wrote it anyway — that the Veil treat the Deep Hollows watch on the sealed door not as criminal mystery but as de facto allied ground, and the Pact's interest in the door as the gravest line of its activity in any city, anywhere. All of that they would file. It was the closing paragraph they would one day hold against her. She wrote it at the fourth bell of night with her amulet on the desk and her hand steady, because a record-keeper's one unbroken vow was to record what was.

*I append a private observation, contrary to instruction, because an assessment that omits it falsifies the assessor. I stood in the dark of that cavern before a door my Church teaches me conceals only heresy's object, ringed by inscriptions no living servant of the Cathedral can read, guarded year upon year by men we call vermin — and what I felt was not the nearness of evil. It was the nearness of something* asked of us, *and unanswered. The Pact reads the deep wrongly, and must be stopped; on this my service is unchanged. But I am no longer able to attest that the Pact has read* nothing. *Something is below. Something has been below a long time. I believe the door knows we have not come to understand it; and I believe — I write it plainly, and stake my office on the distinction — that of all the wills now converging on that place, ours is not yet the most honest.*

She sealed the report into the Veil's eternal files, where it would wait — like the relic in its cache, like the packet at Redspire, like every other letter this age had written and not yet sent — for the reader the times would eventually force to exist. Then she banked her lamp and sat a while in the dark of her cell-office, under six years of string and paper, an inquisitor with a doubt in writing and a hunt with no face on it yet. Far below her, under the markets and the bells and the whole stacked machinery of Everpeak, the cressets burned their plain oil before the black-stone door, and the guards stood facing outward, and the river that had been moved in 1075 went on not flowing, and the door went on being shut.

It is shut at this writing. The honest pray it is patient.
