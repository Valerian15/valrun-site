On the third day of Ambershade, Petra Lowtide was taken off the upriver roster — the good roster, the one with the bore-runs and the fees — and assigned to stand at an old woman's elbow for a festival. She said so, in those words, to the Guild-Sergeant, who let her finish and then said the thing that ended the conversation: "Hara asked for steady arms. Yours are steady. Be at the Vigil dock at grey-light, fourth day, blue cloak, clean."

So it was settled, the way everything in Sea Breeze was settled — by the tide-table and the seniority list — and Petra spent the third day of Ambershade in a fine sulk, hauling trestles with the dock-boys while the whole estuary mouth turned itself out for the one morning a year it did not entirely understand.

It was honest work at least, the preparing. The Vigil dock — the short stone jetty below the pilot-tower that no vessel moored at, all year, by a custom nobody could cite the origin of — was scrubbed, not decorated; the Vigil did not run to bunting. The honey came down through the afternoon from half of Eryndor: crocks and combs and sealed jars, cart after cart, farmwives and temple stewards and a factor from Timbercross with a wax-sealed tithe of forty pots, all of it tallied into the dock-shed by the Guild's clerks as if it were cargo. Which, Petra supposed, it was. The strangest freight in the kingdom: a year of sweetness, paid to the sea.

The rules came with the work, recited by the dock-boys to one another like skipping-rhymes. No vessel at the Vigil dock, that day or any day. No iron on the stones — the trestles were pegged oak, the tally-hooks bronze. No one seaward of the Master once the rite began. And the one that made Petra snort and then think: no payment ever taken for the honey. The factor from Timbercross had tried to leave coin for porterage once, years back, Marrek said, and the Guild had returned it by rider at its own cost, forty leagues, rather than let one Crown touch the freight. "It's not trade," Marrek said. "Trade you can haggle. This you keep exact, or why keep it at all."

"And the sea comes and takes it?" she said. Old Marrek had piloted the bore for forty years and was directing trestles with his stick.

"The sea *receives* it," Marrek said. "Mind your blasphemy near the Master."

Petra had the working pilot's view: the Vigil was dock-theatre. Lovely, old, harmless. One of the deep-folk came up a ladder once a year, waved its arms about, took the honey, and the town felt blessed — and meanwhile the actual sea, the one she worked, went on doing what it did: shoaling where it never used to, running the bore a half-glass off the old tables, drowning men who trusted last year's chart. That was her religion, the daily reading of an unsentimental water, and it had no room in it for honey.

Toward dusk a grain-coaster warped out on the ebb, and her crew sang the departing verse as they went — *Maris, hold the wind for me, Maris, hold the line below* — and the sound came across the harbour in the amber light, and Petra stood with a trestle in her arms and was ambushed, briefly and against her own rules, by the town she belonged to. Then she put the trestle down and went home.

Grey-light, fourth day. Blue cloak. Clean. At least, she thought, falling asleep, the weather would hold. She'd read the sky at sunset, and the sky did not lie.

The sky, as it happened, was the only thing that morning that didn't.

Hara Brinedaughter was awake long before grey-light, as she had been before every Vigil for twenty-five years, and she spent the dark hour as she always spent it: at her table under the lamp, with the notebooks.

Four of them now, beyond the old canon-book. The canon-book was her predecessor's and his predecessor's before that — the Vigil-Master's working text, the gesture-canon drawn hand by hand in fading inks, eighty years of marginalia. The shape of the rite as it had always been. The four new books were her own heresy, begun in 1140: one book a year, the whole Vigil drawn fresh each time, every gesture in order, her own stiff careful sketches of webbed hands — as performed, not as prescribed. A pilot's habit, in the end. You did not navigate by the chart alone. You logged the water you actually crossed, and the difference between chart and log was where the truth lived.

The difference had a shape. Four years of it now.

She laid the books open in a row and went down them again, 1140 to 1143. The rite, entire, unchanged — save in one place. After the ninth position — the gathering of the swell, two hands drawing the weight of water inward — where the canon-book passed directly to the tenth, there was, since 1140, something added. Small, the first year: a turn of one hand, palm down, pressed slowly toward the dock-stones, as if smoothing something. She had nearly not drawn it. In '41, the same, but both hands. In '42, both hands, twice. Last year, both hands twice and then held — Hara's pen had written beside it, *count of six* — and then she looked up at the old woman with the lamp between them and said, "And today?"

"Count of nine," Hara said. "And the light in the arms, twice, brighter than ever I've recorded. Drawn from memory tomorrow, if my hand consents. By you, if it doesn't."

She made the girl earn the books first. The positions, named in order, the way the canon-book had named them for eighty years: the waking of the water, the asking, the answer of the asking, on through the gathering of the swell to the long blessing — Petra repeating each, hands and name together, until the recital stood. "Now you have the chart," Hara said. "Now you can be told where the log leaves it."

They tried the easy explanations first, because Hara insisted — *kill the cheap answers yourself, girl, or they come back* — and Petra, pilot-trained, was good at killing cheap answers. The Tide-Sayer is old; rites drift. But drift scatters, and this accreted: same element, same position, growing by intervals, like a depth called out on a falling tide. Drift doesn't grow by intervals. Signals do. The old woman's eyes are going. But the deviation was in the '40 book, drawn when the eyes were four years younger, and the honey-order was in the clerks' roll this very noon, witnessed by a nineteen-year-old with no faith and good vision. It means nothing; honey is honey. But the markings brightened at the deviation and nowhere else — a body spending light at one word in a long speech — and bodies, Petra knew from reading crews in foul weather, spend at the place that costs.

By midnight there was one explanation left standing, and neither of them would say it grandly, so the pilot said it as pilotage:

"It's a leading-mark." She turned the 1143 book to face the old woman. "That's what this is. Same mark, same bearing, lit brighter every year. We do it with lanterns on the Timbercross bar: when the channel's shifting and we can't yet say where to, we brighten the lead and we hold it, so that anyone who's been logging the lights knows — change coming, stand by for the new line. It's not a sentence, Master. It's not words at all. It's *make ready.*" She sat back, unnerved by her own chart. "Which means it's addressed. You don't burn oil on a lead nobody's watching. Whoever sent that out of the deep thinks somebody up here keeps the log."

The lamp fluttered. Hara looked down at four years of her own stiff drawings — the entire watching apparatus of the surface world, as far as the sea knew or could know: one pilot's heresy in four cheap books — and laughed, once, shortly, without much joy.

"Somebody does," she said. "Two of us, as of tonight. And one elf in a tower." She flexed the left hand on the table, watching it not quite close. "The sea writes to the shore for five years, girl, and this is the post-house it has: an old woman's knuckles. There's your Pressure. There's the weight of the world, right there." She pushed the blank 1144 book across the table. "Sit. While it's fresh in me. Ninth position first — the gathering of the swell — two hands, so. Draw."

The letter to Onhethas was begun the next evening, and for the first time in their four years of correspondence, the hand on the page was not Hara's.

She dictated from the chair by the window, where the light was, with the new book open on her knees — Petra's drawings, cruder than her own and tighter, a manifest-keeper's lines, every frame counted and dated; better than her own, she conceded privately, in everything but love — and the girl wrote at the table, tongue between her teeth, in the round Guild-school hand the clerks beat into apprentices for the lading-bills.

"*To the Lady Yaviel Starbloom, at Onhethas,*" Hara began, "*from H. Brinedaughter, Vigil-Master at Sea Breeze, the fifth day of Ambershade in the year 1144. My lady. You asked me to omit nothing, however small. Herewith the year, and it is not small.*"

She gave it all in order, dock-fashion: the fifth year of the deviation; the count of nine; the brightening, twice, brighter; the changed acceptance-order and the forty travel-pots — "*read off the cargo, my lady, by a Guild apprentice with no prior sight of the rite; I enclose her drawings and commend her eye*" — and then the comparison-night's findings, the cheap answers killed, the leading-mark reading set out plainly as the best surviving chart of the matter. She dictated the way she piloted: soundings and bearings, no flourish. Only at the end, with the harbour going dark outside the window, did she permit the log one entry of the other kind.

"*I am an old woman, my lady, and pious in the Path's fashion, which as you know is the dry fashion. I do not say the sea speaks. I say: for five years something in the deep has held a lamp at the same bearing and trimmed it brighter each Ambershade, and there is no practice of lamps, on water or under it, that does so without intent of a crossing. Something out there is making ready, and has the courtesy — or the need — to say so. The Vigil was never the fossil we kept it for. It was the post-road, kept open three hundred years against the day there was a letter. The letter has begun arriving. I write so that more than my knuckles may hold the fact of it.*"

Petra's pen stopped. "And the close, Master?"

Hara looked out at the dock — her dock, empty under the first stars, the sea-ladder going down into the black water where the year's sweetness had gone — and gave the girl the close, and with it, though neither of them said any word about succession, the thing the whole Vigil had perhaps been for all along: the handing on, witness to witness, of the duty of paying attention.

"*Write: the rite is ended for the year; the log is fair-copied in a steadier hand than mine; and the lamp, my lady, is still burning. — H. B., her mark.*" She worked the stiff fingers once, and made, slowly and with entire deliberation, her mark.

The dock was bare by the sixth day — trestles stowed, shed swept, the clerks' rolls sealed into the Guild archive with three centuries of their kind — and Petra Lowtide walked out along it at slack water, alone, on no errand she could have named.

The harbour was just the harbour: gulls, tar-smell, the bore due at the turn, a coaster working in past the bar on exactly the line the new tables said and the old tables didn't. Nothing on the empty Vigil dock said that anything had happened there. Nothing anywhere said anything, which was, she was discovering, the whole nature of the matter. The world did not announce. It led. It set its small lights at their bearings — a gesture grown by a count, a loading-order changed, an ache in an old woman's hand — and left you to keep the log or miss it, and did not care which. A gull dropped a crab on the dock-stones and ate it. That, she thought, standing where the Tide-Sayer had stood, was what the old faiths had been trying to say with all their weight and water. Not that the sea loved you. That the sea was telling you, always, in the only language it had; and attention was therefore not a virtue but a seamanship.

She had gone up sceptical and come down — not believing; she tested the word and discarded it; Hara would have discarded it too. Watching. She had been read into the log. Next Ambershade she would stand the dock knowing the ninth position from the tenth, with last year's drawings in her satchel and a count kept under her breath, waiting on a count of ten, or twelve, or — the thought arrived with a pilot's plainness — waiting on the year the deviation stopped growing. Every leading-light she had ever steered by burned brightest just before the change of channel. Then the new line was declared. Then you steered it, ready or not.

What was down there, deciding? What had been down there all along, under the honey and the bells and three hundred years of blessings — patient, courteous, making ready? She did not know. The old woman did not know. The elf in the tower, by Hara's account, suspected and did not know. The whole bright surface of the world ran on not knowing, the way a crew sleeps soundly over six hundred fathoms.

The bore came up the estuary at the turn, punctual by the new tables, a grey travelling wall with the evening on it, and Petra Lowtide read it as she had been trained from age nine to read it — height, set, the seethe at the flanks — and found her trained eye doing what it would now do at every water for the rest of her life: logging the difference. Against last year. Against the chart. Against the comfortable belief, which she had carried like every soul in Sea Breeze and would never carry again, that the sea one grew up on is the sea there is.

That was the cost, and she paid it standing on the empty dock, and judged it — being honest by trade — cheap at the price. She was done with children's soundings.

The lamp was still burning. Somebody had to watch it.
