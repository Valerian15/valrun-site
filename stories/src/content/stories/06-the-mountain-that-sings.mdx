The carving had not been there three days ago.

Halvard Tenne stood in the loremasters' hall with his lamp raised, making sure of this, because a loremaster makes sure of things before he says them. He had walked this passage on his morning round for eleven years. He knew the stone. The passage from the Hall of the First Stone to the loremasters' hall had a specific catalogue of marks — the quarry-cut seam at the second niche, the old moisture-track that had left a mineral stain shaped like a closed hand, the patch of smoothed wall where an apprentice had tried to grind out a mis-struck rune two decades ago. He knew these the way he knew the voices of his colleagues at table: without attending.

The new carving was between the mineral stain and the patch of smoothed wall, at precisely the height where it could not be reached without a step-stool. The step-stool in question was kept in the second alcove, which was behind him. He turned and looked at it. It sat exactly where it always sat.

The carving was not a rune from any system Halvard recognised, which meant it was either obscure or novel, and the difference mattered. It was a single mark, deeply cut, with the clean confidence of a tool held by a hand that knew what it was doing. The tool had been something very sharp. The stone was Durumbar granite, which turned tools that were not exactly right for it, and this had not turned at all.

He stood with his lamp for a long time, and then he did what a loremaster does: he went to his workroom and looked it up.

<Break />

The mark was not in the first six references, or the next four, or the deep catalogue of the archive tier that he spent the morning in. He found one thing, in the third volume of the *Stoneheld Marginalia* — a collection of pre-Imperial annotation that his predecessor had spent a career assembling — that was close enough to make his hands stop on the page. It was not a match. But the shape-family was related, and the shape-family was associated with resonance notation.

Resonance notation was not a system Durumbar used any longer. It had been used in the deep-construction period, to mark the acoustic properties of chambers being carved — where the stone sang, where it damped, where a vibration in one chamber would find its sympathetic frequency in another two levels down. The Grimgar compact's engineers had mostly absorbed this knowledge into their structural surveys, and the notation system itself had been retired when the archive tier's catalogue was reorganised in Halvard's grandfather's time on the grounds that it was no longer in active use.

The mark in the passage, if it was a resonance notation, indicated a chamber below.

There was no chamber below.

Halvard checked the structural surveys. He checked the deep-construction records. He checked the archive tier's register of the under-mountain levels, which was six hundred years old and had been verified in the Third Age, and which said, with the settled certainty of things that have been authoritative for a long time: nothing below the fourth level in this section.

He checked the surveys again, because a loremaster makes sure.

The surveys said what they said. The stone said something different, in a mark he could not account for, in a passage where no one had been.

<Break />

Teven Ashrock had been Master of Lore for thirty years and had the quality that long service in authority develops in the best of its holders: an exact sense of the difference between information that required his attention and information that required his decision, and which of those was happening at any given moment. When Halvard brought him to the passage, he stood with his lamp at the same height Halvard had and examined the mark with the same careful silence.

"You've looked it up," he said.

"Resonance notation. Possibly. The match is not exact but the shape-family is."

"Resonance notation for what, exactly."

"A chamber. Below this section."

Teven's lamp held steady. "The surveys."

"Are clear. Nothing below the fourth level here."

"Then the surveys are wrong, or the mark is."

"Yes."

Teven was quiet for a long time. A loremaster who had held his post for thirty years was also, in Durumbar, a dwarf who had held his post for thirty years, which meant he had been formed in the period when the third Velorath Resonance Chamber was the kind of question that was not asked aloud in the presence of people whose answers you could not vouch for. The chamber's existence was not a secret. It was simply something that the mountain knew and the survey-books did not say.

"I'm going to tell you something," Teven said, "that is not in the archive. Not because it was suppressed, but because the decision was made, when I held your post, that some knowledge should live in persons rather than paper, for the same reason the mountain keeps its own records in stone and not in ink."

Halvard waited.

"The third Velorath Resonance Chamber is real. It is below us, in this section, at a depth the structural surveys do not show. It was discovered in the second year of the seventh century by a survey team that included my teacher's teacher. The decision not to enter it in the survey-books was made by the Council of Loremasters at that time on the grounds that the chamber had not been surveyed internally, that its extent and stability were unknown, and that including an unverified chamber in the authoritative survey would either invite approach before it was safe or require falsifying the verification record."

"So the surveys are not wrong," Halvard said slowly. "They simply do not include what they have not verified."

"The surveys are accurate to the range of the survey. The range of the survey does not include the third chamber. This is consistent. The mark—" Teven looked at it again. "The mark is not consistent with anything I know. No apprentice cut that, and no journeyman, and the only tool that would sit in Durumbar granite like that is from the deep-construction period."

"Four hundred years ago."

"At minimum."

They both stood looking at it. The mark had been cut by something that did not belong to the last four centuries, in a passage that Halvard had walked for eleven years, three days ago.

<Break />

The decision to investigate was made by the Council of Loremasters, because Teven Ashrock was not a man who made decisions that belonged to councils. What was not made by council was the composition of the investigation team, which Teven assembled himself on the grounds that the matter required people who could be trusted to record accurately what they found and to say nothing they had not recorded. This meant people with specific qualities, and specificity of quality did not always align with seniority or political balance.

He chose Halvard, who had found the mark. He chose a structural specialist named Renne who had spent her career in the deep-construction records and could read resonance notation's older forms. He chose a journeyman stone-carver of twenty-four who had never been on an investigation team before, because the journeyman had, in three years in the archive tier, demonstrated the most important quality: he noticed things that did not belong and said so, regardless of who else was in the room.

The approach to the third chamber, when they found it — Renne found it, on the third day of systematic sounding, in the small hall off the Hall of the First Stone that the structural surveys showed as a utility alcove — was through three meters of virgin rock that had been opened and closed in the deep-construction period and had never since been touched.

Or had been opened in a way the surveys did not record. The distinction was becoming, by the fourth day, a technical question Halvard had to hold carefully separate from the larger one.

The chamber itself was twenty meters across, roughly circular, its walls covered in the resonance notation of the deep-construction period in a density none of them had seen outside of the archive tier's reference plates. It was, structurally, fine — Renne spent two hours on the walls and came back saying it was better than fine, that it had been carved with an acoustic intention she could identify but not fully read with what she had. There was a fracture in the north face, old and stable, that had been there since the construction period. There was nothing in it that should not have been in it.

Except one thing.

In the center of the chamber floor, in a style that was neither the deep-construction period's nor any modern school, in the same sharp cut that had made the mark in the passage, were five symbols. Renne crouched over them for a long time. When she stood, her face had the expression it wore when she was composing what she would say.

"These are new," she said. "The stone's fresh." She looked at Halvard. "Very fresh. The chip-edges are still sharp. Within the month."

The journeyman stone-carver was on one knee, close to the symbols, and he said: "These are right. Whatever they're saying, they're saying it right. Whoever cut these knew the language."

<Break />

Teven Ashrock wrote his report in his own hand, on the principle that a document of this weight should come from the hand that could be held accountable for it, and he wrote it accurately, which meant it contained everything that had been found, including the things he could not explain. The chamber's existence. Its condition. The fresh symbols in a system that should not have been accessible to any living hand. The structural implication of the symbols, which Renne had, on the fifth day, worked out enough of to say: *the fracture in the north face is part of a larger stress pattern; the symbols are marking the stress pattern; the stress pattern, if it develops, will reach the fourth-level surveys' range in somewhere between fifteen and two hundred years.*

The range was not satisfying. It was accurate.

The report went to the council, which received it with the specific quality of silence that signals a body encountering a situation it has not previously categorized. The council had procedures for new structural findings. It had procedures for deep-archive investigations. It had no procedure for a chamber that had known it was about to be found and left a message about it, in a language no living loremaster had used, in a hand that should not have been there.

What the council did, in the end, was the thing councils do when faced with something they do not have a category for: it created one. A commission of investigation, ongoing, with a remit to examine the symbols, the stress pattern, the carving history, and the question of who or what had left the mark in the passage. It placed Halvard at its head, because he had found it. It placed Renne on it, because she could read it further. It did not place Teven on it, because Teven was Master of Lore and the commission would eventually report to him, and the same person should not sit on both sides of an inquiry.

Teven wrote, at the end of the report's summary:

*I have been in service in this mountain for fifty-three years. The mountain is several hundred million years older than that, and the interval during which we have been learning to read its records properly is considerably shorter than my service. I report what was found. I make no claim about what it means that it was found now, except this: the mountain does not wait. When the mountain is ready to be heard, it marks the passage.*

He filed the report in the archive tier under the survey's expanded record. He filed a copy in the system of knowledge-in-persons, which is to say: he told twelve people who could be trusted to hold it, and instructed each of them to tell two more, each chosen with care, so that the knowledge would survive the loss of any individual carrier. This was how the third Velorath Resonance Chamber had been kept for three hundred years, before the survey-books were made to agree with what they had verified.

The commission continues. The symbols are being read, slowly, one correct reading at a time. The fracture in the north face is being monitored on a cycle that Renne designed before she was promoted to Master of Survey, and the cycle's records are the most carefully maintained in the mountain. The mark in the passage remains. Halvard will not permit it to be removed or covered. He says it is part of the record and the record should be visible.

He walks past it each morning on his round, and does not attend it, the way he does not attend the closed hand of the mineral stain or the smoothed patch of the mis-struck rune. It is part of the passage now. He knows it the way he knows the others: without attending, which is how you know the things that will not leave.
