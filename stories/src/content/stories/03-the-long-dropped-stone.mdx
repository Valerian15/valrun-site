The testament in question had been misfiled for four hundred years, and the only reason the second prince of Aureon found it was that he had been assigned the ninth gallery for his archival service and the ninth gallery was where things went when they could not be made to fit anywhere else.

"It was under the L's," said Arven, the librarian's third assistant, who had accompanied the prince with the embarrassed helpfulness of someone managing a problem that might become protocol. "Bound with the Lokhari treaties. Wrong period, wrong subject, wrong shelf."

"What's the correct shelf?" the prince asked.

"We are not entirely — there is no complete register for the gallery's pre-Fourth Age holdings." Arven paused. "There is a register. It is incomplete."

"What percentage complete?"

A longer pause. "Perhaps sixty. The rest was a water event in the Second Age, your grace, and the record of what was lost—"

"Was also lost. Yes." The prince held the testament to the light. The sealing wax was old enough to have gone through two colour changes and arrived at the grey of deep ash. He had found it this morning tucked behind the L bindings, flat against the shelf's back board, face-down. "What do you know about Covenant law as it pertains to suppressed testamentary instruments?"

Arven blinked. "Very little, your grace. Is it — should I send for the legal curator?"

The prince looked at the seal. It bore an imprint he recognized, because the study of pre-Third Age administrative heraldry was one of his stranger enthusiasms, and he had a good memory. He set the testament on the table.

"Fetch whoever in this building knows the most about the Zelkaris line," he said. "Don't tell them why."

<Break />

The Zelkaris line had been dead for six centuries and was in any case a subject for court historians rather than legal scholars, so the archive sent him the court historian, a woman of sixty named Senna Varath who arrived with a satchel and the expression of someone who has long since stopped being surprised by the things royalty turned up.

She looked at the seal and said nothing for a moment. Then she set down her satchel.

"Where was it found?"

"Ninth gallery. Behind the L bindings, face-down."

"L."

"L. Below a shelf-mark that reads: *Lok, treaties, Tertiary.* The binding it was pressed against is dated twelve years before the Third Age. This is not a Lokhari treaty."

Senna Varath turned the testament over with both hands, careful, the way one turned things one was not certain one should be touching. "The seal is genuine. I won't be sure of the document inside until it's opened under the proper conditions, but the seal — I know this impression." She looked up at him. "Are you aware of the Zelkaris Compact succession dispute?"

"In the form in which it appears in the general histories."

"The form in which it appears in the general histories omits the third claimant."

<Break />

The third claimant was a name the general histories had lost or dropped, and the reason they had lost it was the reason Senna Varath spent the next two days very carefully explaining to the prince, who read the unsealed testament himself rather than taking a summary.

The testament was from the compact's second year — the year the succession had been resolved in favour of the line that had held it ever since, the line that had become the imperial family of Aureon, his family — and it was in the hand of the compact's presiding authority, whose seal it bore, and it said, in the plain declarative language of post-Third Age administrative record, that the evidence offered in support of the first claimant's legitimacy had included a document that the presiding authority, upon examination, had found to be of uncertain provenance.

Not forged. Uncertain provenance. The careful language of a man doing something very precise.

"Which document?" the prince asked.

"The Zelkarun birth registry entry for the disputed year." Senna Varath had spread her notes across the table with the deliberate calm of someone who has arranged an unpleasant display and does not intend to make it worse by hurrying. "The original registry was lost with the island. What survived were copies, of which there were three. Two supported the first claimant. One, found in the Ash Archive, supported the second."

"My ancestor."

"Your ancestor. The compact resolved in her favour, and the basis of that resolution was — as it has been recorded — the weight of the documentary evidence."

"Which was two to one against her," the prince said.

"Yes."

"And the presiding authority found the original registry uncertain."

"The two documents supporting your ancestor's claim."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the deep archive's ventilation ducts breathed in the unhurried rhythm of a building that had never expected to be relevant.

"If the testament had been registered," the prince said, "what would have been the legal consequence, in the compact's own terms?"

"Re-examination. Possibly re-determination. The compact's language gave the presiding authority — he died without registering it; that's the piece I can't account for — the power to reopen a determination within the first decade if new evidence of procedural failure came to light." Senna Varath's hands lay flat on the table. "The first decade expired in the compact's eleventh year. We are six hundred and forty-six years past that."

"So it is moot."

"Legally, entirely. Your family has occupied the throne of Aureon for six centuries. The testament establishes, at most, that the succession question was handled with less certainty than the official record suggests. It establishes no current claim, no legal liability, nothing actionable." She paused. "It establishes a fact, your grace, about how the archive was used six centuries ago. Whether that fact is worth — what you do with it is your question, not mine."

<Break />

He spent four days with the document before he sent for anyone, and the first person he sent for was not a legal officer but his brother, because his brother was the heir and what the prince was deciding was not, in the end, a legal question.

The heir was twenty-two and steady and had their mother's practical turn, and he listened to the full explanation with no expression that the prince could read. Then he said:

"What do you want to do with it?"

"I don't know yet. That's why I'm telling you."

"If you register it, it becomes an archive record. Someone will find it in fifty years and write a monograph. Another fifty years, someone writes a second monograph that says the first one exaggerated. In two hundred years, a faction footnotes it. In four hundred, it's background." The heir turned the testament over, looking at the back. "If you suppress it, you know what you are."

"Yes."

"If you resign the succession—"

"On a document six centuries moot that doesn't establish a legal claim—"

"The reason doesn't have to be legal," the heir said. "You've never been comfortable with it. I've known this since we were children. I thought it was a temperamental thing. Now I think you were waiting for something to hang it on."

The prince said nothing.

"That's not a condemnation," the heir said. "I'm not arguing with you. I'm saying: if this is the reason you were waiting for, take it. Register the document in the archive and write your own statement into the formal record. A clear statement. Not 'circumstances have led me to reconsider.' Something that says plainly what the document says and what you believe about it. And step down." He paused. "I'd rather have a brother than an emperor, honestly. I've always thought you were unsuited to the title, in the best possible sense."

The prince found, somewhat to his surprise, that the decision was already made and had been made, probably, since the moment he turned the testament over and recognized the seal. The four days had not been deciding. They had been preparing.

"The Bastion's Reach appointment," he said. "The harbour administration post. That is still open?"

"I'll check."

"I want something with numbers in it. Manifests, ledgers — not policy."

"I'll find something with numbers," the heir said, and the case with which he said it suggested he had, in fact, been waiting as well.

<Break />

The formal statement went into the imperial archive under the current emperor's hand — the brother, who had moved from heir to throne with the efficiency of a man who had done the paperwork for it already — and the prince's accompanying statement was what he had promised his brother it would be: plain, specific, declarative. What the testament said. What he believed about it. Why he was stepping down. No calls for re-examination, no legal claims, no accusations. A record, left in the place records went.

He went to Bastion's Reach and administered the harbour for twelve years, which was among the more useful things any prince had done in the imperial period, and the records from his tenure there are still used in the harbour courts as the baseline for tariff calculation because he was extremely good with numbers. He was never emperor. He was, by most accounts, at least as well-regarded in Bastion's Reach as his brother was in the palace, and with the advantage that the harbour's opinion of a man was based on whether the manifests cleared.

The testament was registered in the ninth gallery of the Aureon imperial archive in the year 656. The legal curator filed it under a new heading — *Disputed instruments, Zelkaris succession, Third Age* — which sat on the shelf next to the Lokhari treaties and could thereafter be found by anyone who knew to look. Senna Varath wrote a monograph. Someone wrote a second monograph fifty years later. The archive went on as archives do, and the document sat in its case in the ninth gallery, which was where things went when they could not be made to fit anywhere else, doing what documents do when they have been properly kept.

They wait.
