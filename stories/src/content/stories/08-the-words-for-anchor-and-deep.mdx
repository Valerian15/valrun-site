The climb was the easy part. Seve had been climbing the wrong things since she could walk, and the Onyx Spires' eastern face — the Tablet Spire in particular, which had a seam of differential hardness running up its middle that made hand-holds if you knew where to press — was the kind of problem she enjoyed: technical, requiring attention, punishing mistakes immediately and proportionally.

The lee pocket at the seam's midpoint was where she took her rest and ate half her food and looked out over the black plain and the grey morning sky and thought about names.

She had three. The first was the one she had been born with, which she did not use. The second was Seve, which she had given herself at fourteen in the standard fashion and which did the work names did in normal life. The third she did not have yet, which was why she was on the Spires at the turn of the tide.

The Bastion Thieves — the loose and self-organizing guild that operated in the warren around the Lower Spires and the western anchorage and occasionally in the Black Bastion's gate-court itself — did not take oaths in the usual form. They had a different economy of trust, one that had been developed over the two hundred years since the Impact by people who had specific reasons not to trust the forms that the world before the Impact had relied on. The third name was earned, not given; it was the name a Thief took from the Spires themselves, in the manner the senior members described as: *go up and read and come back and say what you found.* Half the guild had never attempted it. A third of those who attempted it came back saying they had found nothing, which was held to be an honest finding and adequately honoured. The ones who brought back words were the ones who had learned to read, or the ones the Spires had chosen to teach.

Seve was not certain she believed in the Spires choosing. She was certain she was going up anyway, and that the uncertainty was, in the Thieves' accounting, the correct attitude.

<Break />

The square chamber was in the Tablet Spire's upper third, behind what appeared from the exterior to be a sealed face and was, on close inspection, a sealed face with a working aperture at its base that was specifically the wrong size for a careless person and the right size for someone who had studied the face first.

She studied the face first. The aperture was forty centimeters across and opened into blackness. She lit her lamp, reduced her pack to what could fit beside her through the gap, and went in.

The chamber was the size of a small room, its walls covered floor to ceiling in marks she did not know, cut in the black glass that the Spires were made of with the same patient confidence she had seen on the stones in the Ash Archive — the stones the Thieves moved sometimes and the Sketh moved back, which was one of the ongoing negotiations of the warren that nobody was in a position to formalize. The floor was clear and dry and clean in the way that enclosed spaces are clean when nothing has entered them in a long time.

She took out her copy-paper and her stick of chalk and began making marks.

She had been taught, by the Thief who had agreed to prepare her, the rudiments of Zelkaris — not the spoken tongue, which was gone with its speakers, but the written notation, which survived in the Ash Archive's stones and in the Spires themselves and in the memory of the people who had learned pieces of it from the Sketh, who learned pieces of it from the people who had learned it from the Sketh, a chain of partial transmission that went back to the Impact. The preparation had taken nine months and had not, she was now aware, been preparation for reading. It had been preparation for knowing what she was looking at.

The marks on the south wall were structural — she could identify the notation-family without being able to read it, the way a person can recognise a script without knowing the language. These were about the Spire itself: its composition, its history, the event that had made it. She left them for later and moved to the east wall, which was different.

The east wall was in two notations. The lower section was the same structural family as the south. The upper section was something she had seen before, because the Thief who prepared her had shown her a rubbing from a stone in the archive — one of the working records, not the formal ones — and said: *this is the language of the old engineers; it is not Zelkaris writing, it is Anchor working notation, and the two things are not the same.*

She worked through the afternoon and into the lamp's second supply, and by the time she had the east wall copied, she had found two words she could read.

<Break />

The first word was not a surprise. The preparation had covered it. *Veth*, the notation for the Anchor's primary working depth — the technical term for the level at which the engine was designed to operate. She had seen it on the archive rubbings. It appeared here in a sequence with several terms she could not parse, but the shape of the sentence was, even to her partial reading, a measuring statement. Something had been measured at *veth* and the measurement was being recorded.

The second word was the surprise. It appeared twice in the upper section of the east wall, both times in a context that her partial reading suggested was comparative — this against that, before and after, a relationship between two things. The word was *surath*, which the Thief had given her with the note that it appeared in the archive stones and had been tentatively translated as *deep* or *below design* — below the level the engine was designed for.

She copied both contexts as exactly as she could, and then sat in the lamp's circle for a long time.

If the measuring statement was saying what she thought it was saying — if *veth* was the designed operating level and *surath* was below that level, and if the east wall was comparing the two — then what was being recorded was either a planned descent below the engine's design depth, or an observation that the engine had gone there already, and the difference between those two readings was the difference between an instruction and a warning.

She was not a scholar of the Anchor. She was a Thief who had spent nine months learning to know what she was looking at, which was exactly far enough to know that she was not qualified to make this call. She copied the east wall complete and packed her kit and went back through the aperture and down the Spire's face in the dark, which was harder than the ascent and took until nearly dawn.

<Break />

She brought the copies to the Thief who had prepared her, whose name was Orvath and whose standing in the guild was of the kind that was reckoned in decades of service rather than in titles. Orvath read the copies in silence for a long time, which was not his habit, and when he looked up his face had the quality that her experience with him had taught her to read as: *this requires people I trust more than my own judgment.*

"Where did you find the east wall?" he said.

"Tablet Spire. The square chamber behind the sealed upper face. I'll take you there."

"I know the chamber." He was still looking at the copies. "I could not read the east wall. I've been in that chamber three times and I knew the upper section was different from the south wall but I could not — nine months of preparation and you got further than twelve years of mine."

"You weren't prepared for Anchor notation," she said. "Neither was I, specifically, but you showed me the archival rubbing."

"I showed you that rubbing because I thought it was relevant to the general preparation." He set the copies down. "I don't know why I thought that. I've been asking myself since you left." He looked at her. "Seve. If the second word means what the tentative translation says—"

"I know."

"The archive translation is tentative because the scholars who worked on it in the Sixth Age did not have sufficient comparative context. What you've copied might be that context."

"I know that too."

"This can't stay with the guild."

She had arrived at the same place during the descent, somewhere between the lee pocket and the ground, in the dark. "The Sketh," she said. "The ones in the archive. The ones who've been translating."

"Not translating. Recording, and waiting for context. Yes." Orvath looked at the copies again. "These go to the archive. You take them."

"I'm not — I'm not a known figure to the archive."

"You're about to be." He picked up his pen and began writing something, which he handed to her: a letter, sealed with the guild's private mark. "Give this to the keeper of the eastern stones. Tell them Orvath sends what the Spire finally gave. They'll know what that means."

She took the letter. She looked at the copies one more time — two words that might mean something, in a context she could partially read, found in a chamber she had gone up to prove herself and come down carrying something she had not expected. She thought about the third name, which was what she had been climbing for.

She had not brought back what she expected. She had brought back what the Spire had, which was different. The question was whether the Thieves' accounting allowed for that kind of substitution.

"Does this count?" she asked. "As finding something."

Orvath looked at her for a long moment. "What did you bring back?"

"Two words I can read and a context I can't fully parse and a question for the scholars."

"That," said Orvath, "is exactly what the Spires have." He took his seal from his belt and pressed it into the letter's wax, which he had not done when he handed it to her. "Your third name is yours to take. Go to the archive. Tell them what you found."

<Break />

The keeper of the eastern stones received the letter and the copies and looked at Seve for the length of time that Sketh look at things they are deciding about, which is longer than humans are comfortable with and means something different from what humans assume.

Then the keeper took the copies to the inner reading room and did not come back for two days, during which Seve slept in the guest space the Bastion Thieves maintained at the western anchorage and thought about what it meant to have given something to the archive.

It meant, she decided, that the thing was no longer just hers. It meant the thing was now in the system of keeping, which was the system that had been running since before the Impact, and which would outlast her and outlast the guild and would presumably outlast the question of what the east wall meant, and would keep the copies until there was enough context to read them fully and report what had been found.

She thought about this without resentment. The archive was what it was. The keeping was what the keeping was. She had been raised by the Thieves, who had a specific and practical relationship with the idea that things went missing and came back and were not always more or less than they had been when they left, and this seemed to her a correct way to understand what had happened in the square chamber: something had been missing long enough to become what was being kept, and it had found, on a specific morning in 1141, the person who could carry it as far as the archive.

The keeper came back on the third day and sat with her in the reading room.

"The second word," the keeper said, in the careful Common of someone translating across something older than Common. "The translation *deep* or *below design*. This is a translation that was incomplete. The full meaning in the context of the working notation is *below the threshold at which the design holds true.* Below the line past which the engine behaves in ways the design did not intend." A pause. "You brought back two words. The second word is the one that changes the first. The measuring statement on the east wall is recording that the engine passed that line."

Seve sat with this for a moment. "Before the Impact."

"Before the Impact. Yes." The keeper set the copies on the table. "We have been holding the south wall's structural record for a hundred years. What you brought is the east wall's operational record. Together they are the account the engineers left. We did not have the account until now."

She went to the Bastion later that day — the gate-court, which she knew, and the archive hall, which she had been in before on the Thieves' business. She stood in the ash-court and looked at the Spires through the gate and thought about what it meant that the engineers had left an account in the one place that could not be entered without the preparation, and wondered whether the Sketh's presence in the archive, holding the record for a hundred years, had been intended by the engineers as a kind of patience-test for whoever came to read it.

She could not resolve this and did not try. She had her third name, taken by agreement with the guild on the morning she returned, which was the name the Spires had given her in the way the Spires gave things — not as a gift, not as an award, but as a consequence of having gone up and come down with what was there.

The third name was *Surath*. It was the right name.
