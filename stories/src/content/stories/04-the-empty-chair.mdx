The longest-reigning emperor in the history of Aureon had governed for six hundred and eighteen years, and the main thing this meant, on the morning of his death, was paperwork.

Not metaphorical paperwork. The literal, administrative, sealed-and-witnessed sort: the transfer of seven hundred and forty-three standing orders, three ongoing treaty processes, four pending appointments, one border adjustment in the second stage of review, the unresolved question of the northern circuit courts' winter jurisdiction, and the entire standing apparatus of an empire that had been organised, for six centuries, around the assumption that the same person would be available to answer questions about it. The emperor's private secretary had been preparing the transfer documents for sixty years on the assumption that eventually they would be needed. They filled twelve cases.

The emperor's daughter had been reading them for three days before the formal mourning period was finished.

Her name was Irenne, and she was two hundred and twelve years old, and she was the heir in the way that the heir to a very long reign becomes the heir: not by imminence but by elimination. Three older siblings were dead; one had died in the second century of the reign, one in the fourth, one in the fifth and most recently. Between her and the throne there was nothing but the paperwork and the question she had been sitting with since her father's last coherent night, which was not a political question or a legal question but the plainest one available: whether she was willing to become the thing the chair required.

The chair was, in fact, also giving her problems. It had been adjusted for her father's use over such a long period that it fit a man six inches shorter and thirty pounds heavier, and the herald's office had sent three carpenters with three contradictory sets of instructions, and the chair was currently in a side room under a cloth while the throne question worked its way up through channels.

She was sitting on a bench in the archive wing, reading transfer documents.

<Break />

The sentence she could not put down was at the bottom of the fourth case, in a section titled *Exceptional Instruments and Outstanding Determinations*, which was the archive's name for the category of things that had been decided but not, for various reasons, acted upon.

It was dated three hundred and forty years ago, in her father's own hand, and it said:

*I find myself persuaded that the form of succession agreed in the compact of the Third Age is historically contingent rather than constitutively necessary; and that the question of whether the throne of Aureon should continue in its current form, with its current powers, or whether the conditions of its continuance should be examined afresh, is not properly mine to resolve alone. I note this here so that the archive holds a record that I considered it. I do not act on it because I cannot determine the right instrument of action, and I will not act on it wrongly. This is not a postponement indefinitely.*

She read it eight times. She had been doing this for a day.

The legal curator, who had accompanied her through the transfer documents and was the kind of useful person who stayed in rooms without being asked, looked up once from his own work.

"If you want my interpretation," he said, "it is not a directive. There's no prescription. He could not bind his successor without one."

"He could not bind himself," she said. "He was still emperor when he wrote it. He wrote it in the category of things that were not acted upon."

"Yes."

"He decided it was a question without deciding an answer."

"That is one accurate reading."

"And left it here for — for the next person." She set the document down. "Did you know it was in the fourth case?"

"I catalogued the cases last year. Yes."

"Did you consider — no. I know you considered whether to tell me. You decided to let me find it."

The legal curator was old enough to have his own opinions about the difference between the two approaches, and he said nothing, which was, in its way, an answer.

<Break />

She thought about it for six more days, which she spent publicly in the forms of mourning and privately in reading everything in the archive connected to the compact of the Third Age, the succession dispute, the record of the second prince's resignation, and the three monographs that had been written about all of it in the intervening centuries.

The monographs were unhelpful in the way that scholarly argument is unhelpful when one is trying to make a decision rather than understand a debate: each of them was careful, evidenced, and at the end recommended more research. The second prince's formal statement was clearer. She read it twice, identified the quality she admired in it — the plainness, the unwillingness to dress the thing up as anything other than what it was — and set it back in its case.

On the seventh day she asked to see the chair, which had been restored and was waiting in the throne room. She stood in the middle of the room and looked at it for a long time, alone, which she had arranged deliberately and which the herald's office had resisted and then, correctly reading her face at breakfast, acceded to.

The chair was large and very old and had had six hundred and eighteen years of continuous use, which gave it a presence that newer furniture did not. It had been repaired and re-upholstered many times and was in its current form nothing like the chair her father had first sat in, except in the location and the name. Succession was, she thought, essentially what the chair was: a continuous thing made of discontinuous repairs.

She could sit in it. She was two hundred and twelve years old and in good health and had, unlike her father's earlier heirs, lived to see the edge of the question. The empire needed a head. The transfer documents needed signing. The north's winter jurisdiction was going to cause trouble by spring if it was not resolved. These were real things.

Her father had spent three centuries, she knew from the archive, putting down rebellions rooted in the question he had recorded in the fourth case. The fourth century's rebellion had been the worst; forty years of civil fracture; sixty thousand dead. He had won, each time, and the winning had been violent, and after the winning the question had gone back into the category of not-acted-upon, where it sat, and accumulated, and waited for the next person.

She could resolve to act on it. That was not the same as knowing how. Her father had not known how, or had been unable to determine it, or — she had been working on this reading for a day — had known something about the how that made acting on it in his own reign impossible, something he could only record and pass forward.

What he had passed forward was the record that he had considered it. The record, placed where she would find it, that the question was legitimately open and had been seen to be open by the throne itself. The legitimacy was the instrument. This was what she had been sitting with.

<Break />

She sat in the chair on the twenty-first day, by which point the herald's office had settled the question of its adjustment and the transfer documents were half-signed and the north's jurisdiction question was producing letters, and she did this in the public form because the public form was what was required and she had been raised to know when requirements were the right instrument.

The court assembled. The forms were performed. She was emperor in the way that a person becomes emperor in a continuous monarchy: by being the person in the chair when the ceremony says there should be one.

Afterward, in the archive wing, she added a record to the fourth case in her own hand:

*I have read this entry and I intend to act on it. My father could not determine the right instrument. I cannot determine it either, on this day. I record my intention so that the archive holds it, because I have learned from the fourth case that the archive is what holds things between the people who carry them. This is not a postponement indefinitely.*

She used the same phrase because it was true, and because the archive would therefore hold two people who had meant it.

The north's jurisdiction question was resolved by spring. The instrument she spent the next forty years looking for, she did not find. What she found, at the end of it, was the instrument's shape: not a decree, not a reform, not a revolution, but an accumulation of small changes — the kind that require a very long reign to compound into something different from what came before. Her father's four centuries of putting-down had been, in their way, a clearing. What she was making, in the space he had left, was something that would take longer than she had to finish. She recorded this too, in the fourth case, in the last year she was well. She noted the shape, and what had been accomplished in the direction of it, and what remained. She noted the people she trusted to carry it.

The case is in the archive. The archive is in Everpeak. The throne is not what it was.
