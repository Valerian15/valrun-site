The petitioner was a human sail-mender named Corra, and her case took the harbour court of Nilvelond a quarter of a glass to decide. Yrsa, clerk of the third bench, recorded every word of it in a hand the registrars held up to apprentices as the standard of the office.

The matter was ordinary. The woman's late husband, a Verdure rope-factor resident eleven years in the port, had died intestate under the Living Law, which made his stock — cordage, a half-share in a drying-loft, a strongbox of mixed coin and four Glimmers — subject to the court's determination. The determination, delivered by the assessor in the melodic formality the bench used for guests, was forfeiture in trust: the estate to be held by the court in trust for such claims as may arise; the widow to receive a dwelling-allowance; the file to be sealed. Lawful. Orderly. The woman did not understand a word of the form of it, and understood the whole of it perfectly, the way the poor always did: she had come in owning her husband's life, and she went out owning an allowance.

Yrsa recorded the widow's face along with the judgment. Not on the page — the page took what the form allowed — but in the place she had kept such records since girlhood, the unpaged ledger behind her eyes, where the entries had lately begun to total themselves whether she willed it or not.

She was thirty-two years old, half-elven, the best clerk on the third bench, and a guest under the law she administered. Her collar carried no bloodline-thread; clerks of mixed parentage wore plain linen, a courtesy of the court's livery that said, with Twiland's exquisite manners, *we will not embarrass you by marking what you lack.* She had been raised in the city's human quarter, schooled on its mixed wharves, and had worked her way into the court's service on penmanship and procedure. She believed in procedure — that must be set down at the start, because everything that follows grew from it. Procedure was the one country that had ever granted her citizenship. The forms did not care what her collar lacked. The forms cared whether the entry was true.

Which was why, that evening, doing the day's filing, she noticed.

The widow's forfeiture went, by form, to the trust-rolls: the court's public register of held estates, audited yearly, claimable by heirs. Yrsa drew the trust-roll volume to make the cross-entry and found no folio waiting. No claim-number had been issued. The file's seal-docket directed the estate not to the rolls at all but to a destination she had inscribed a hundred times without reading it, the way one stops reading one's own street's name: *to the Vault of the Vine, for keeping.*

The Vault of the Vine was a bloodline vault. Thornroot. Private. Bloodline vaults did not appear in the trust-rolls, were not audited by the court, and could not, by their nature, ever be claimed against by a sail-mender's heirs.

She checked four more forfeiture files that night, going back a year. Then ten, going back five. The entries were all in order. That was the thing she carried home through the lamp-lit terraces, the finding that would unmake and remake her life: every entry was in order, and the order was the crime.

The Vault of the Vine opened at the second hour after sunrise and closed at the second hour after noon, and between those hours its warden, Telvar Thornroot, conducted what he privately knew to be a liturgy and publicly called the counting.

He was Thornroot by blood — the collar-thread was real, embroidered fine — and Telvar's magic had come in weak. Not absent; worse: measurable. A Thornroot of absent gift might have been a scandal, a story. A Thornroot who could light a Glimmer-lamp on the third attempt was an embarrassment of arithmetic, a decimal place in the line's purity, and the line had done with him what old lines did with their decimals — placed him where blood was required and power was not. A vault wants a Thornroot at its door, for the dignity of the depositors. It does not want one who might do anything. Telvar had kept the Vault of the Vine for forty years, and the appointment was, as everyone understood and no one said, a verdict he was serving rather than an office he held.

He kept it perfectly. That was his answer to the verdict — the only answer the verdict permitted — and the vault under his hands had become the most exactly ordered three hundred spans in Nilvelond: the deposit-counters of black walnut; the drawer-galleries in their rosewood ranks, bloodline holdings to the east, commercial pledges to the west; his counters and registers balancing to the Glimmer, season upon season. The depositors saw a minor functionary of pleasant manner. The vault saw the truest Thornroot of his generation, if keeping is a bloodline's work.

And the vault had a wing that was never counted.

The north gallery. Lawful — everything was lawful; the deposits came under court seal-dockets, *for keeping*, and a warden does not audit the court. But the north gallery appeared in no manifest he was required to balance, received no depositor's visits, issued no releases, and grew. Drawer by drawer, year by year: strongboxes with the guild-marks of dead human tradesmen; trade-stock tokens; pouches of Glimmers tagged in clerks' hands; and the bloodline tokens. A dozen of them now. The carved heart-wood discs that elven lines gave their children, surrendered or stripped or renounced in proceedings he never saw, lying in a Thornroot drawer like extracted teeth. One drawer among them he had begun, fourteen years past, to re-lock differently — his small wordless ceremony of unease.

He did not ask. Forty years he had not asked. A weak-magic Thornroot in a granted office did not ask; asking was for men whose standing could absorb an answer.

But he counted. Everything in his keeping, the never-audited with the audited, in a private register he kept inside the official one the way a true thought lives inside a courtesy — because the verdict had left him exactly one dignity, and he would not be the warden of an uncounted thing. And at the deposit-counter, on the days when the harbour court's clerks came down with the season's seal-dockets, he had begun to notice that one of them — the half-elven one from the third bench, the one with the plain collar and the unforgiving hand — had begun, while he processed her dockets, to read his counters upside down.

He should have reported it. He re-ordered his keys instead, and found, with a shock he filed under nothing at all, that he was looking forward to the next docket-day.

The counter between them was black walnut, a span and a half wide, and across it, through the autumn and winter of 1131, the two most precise people in Nilvelond conducted the slowest conversation in the history of the port.

It was made of procedure entirely. She brought the third bench's dockets; he received them. Seal checked against register, item against schedule, her signature, his counter-signature — a transaction of perhaps a hundred words, all prescribed. The conversation lived underneath, in the choices the forms left open. She always read the schedule aloud rather than passing it silent, so that he could counter-check by ear, which respected his counting. He always issued her receipt unasked, before she could request it, which respected her time. When a docket's arithmetic was wrong — the bench's error, never hers — each had learned to say so to the paper, not the person: "The schedule wants correction," not "you are mistaken." Two functionaries of no standing, meeting at the one altitude where neither was a guest or a decimal: the work, done exactly.

He learned her, docket-day by docket-day, the way he learned a register: by entries. That she was court-trained but wharf-raised — her Common had the port under it, and once, when a Verdure skipper at the next counter mangled the deposit-forms, she translated for him into dockside cant without being asked, fluent, warm, and then resumed her court voice like a clerk re-sharpening a quill. That she was kind in the only register the law allowed her: procedurally — he watched her walk a frightened human depositor through a release-form three times, never varying from the prescribed words, only slower each time, softer each time, until the form itself became a kindness. That she missed nothing. Her eyes crossed his counters, his keys, his drawer-galleries the way his own crossed a manifest. Not curious. Auditing.

She learned him the same way. That the vault's order was not fussiness but a made thing, a forty-year argument with someone — she recognised the species of it, having an unpaged ledger of her own. That his line's thread sat on his collar like a sentence being served; she had watched two young Thornroots of the pure gift come once with a deposit, and watched the warden's flawless deference, and read, in the space of one deposit, across a walnut counter, the whole biography Nilvelond had agreed not to discuss. That his counter-signature, on every receipt, was full and fair and slightly larger than required — a man insisting, in the only field left to him: I am here; this at least is mine. That he re-locked one drawer in the north gallery differently from all the others. She had seen the key-ceremony twice through the gallery arch, and filed it.

Guest and decimal. The law had a place for each of them, and the places were adjoining; and all that winter, across a span and a half of black walnut, neither said one word that was not prescribed; and by Thawmere both could have testified — though no court in Twiland had a form for it — that they were known, at last, by someone whose knowing was exact.

<Break />

She found her own name in the spring, on the fourth night of what she had come to call, in the unpaged ledger, the long audit.

She had been pulling forfeiture files for half a year by then — methodically, lawfully, a folio here and a docket there, every request within a third-bench clerk's authority — and she had the custom mapped. The guests' estates that reached the trust-rolls: the ones with advocates, the ones with elven creditors, the ones it would be untidy to disappear. And the ones that went to the Vault of the Vine, for keeping — into the north of the world, beyond audit, beyond claim, beyond form. Thirty-one estates in nine years that she could document. The harbour court of Lord Fenrath Thornroot had been quietly converting the wreckage of guest lives into Thornroot keeping for at least a generation, under seals she had inscribed herself. She did not believe the Lord had ever read one of these dockets. That was the genius of the machine: it required no malice anywhere in it. Only custom, and clerks.

The old index was the last volume of the night, a register of keepings from before her service, requisitioned because her thirty-one wanted a baseline. And there, in a predecessor's hand, in the entries for 1118, she read:

*Vael, mariner, of the human quarter, dec'd: trade-stock and instruments, forfeit in trust; to the Vault of the Vine, for keeping.*

*Same proceeding: one bloodline token (line Moonsong, minor branch), surrendered by Liriel, called Vael's-wife, upon her removal from the line's rolls; to the Vault of the Vine, for keeping.*

Her father's sextant and her mother's name, in one drawer, fourteen years.

She had known the shapes. Children always know the shapes: that her father had drowned the winter she was eighteen; that the court had settled matters; that her mother — disowned long before, for the marriage, by a minor Moonsong branch that could not afford the impurity — had gone silent about it all, drawn her dwelling-allowance, and died within five years, quiet as a closed file. Yrsa had built her life on the far side of those shapes. She had concluded, with an orphan's iron practicality, that the law was the only parent left standing, and had gone to work for it, and had served it brilliantly, believing — believing, that was the word; she made herself write it in the unpaged ledger — that the forms did not care what her collar lacked.

The forms did not care. That was true. The forms had processed her father's life and her mother's name into a rosewood drawer with the same serene completeness they processed everything; and had then hired the daughter, on penmanship, to inscribe the seals on the next thirty-one.

She sat in the registry until the lamps burned out. She did not weep; she itemised — that was how grief took her, she discovered, in schedules — and somewhere toward dawn the itemising arrived at its foot-total, and the total was not rage. Rage she could have filed. It was simpler than rage, and it never afterward left her:

The manifest is false. I am an officer of a false manifest. The entry must be corrected.

<Break />

On the first docket-day of summer she asked him the question, and Telvar knew before she finished it that it was the hinge of his life, because it was technically permitted and no one in forty years had ever asked it.

She laid the season's dockets on the walnut, waited for his counter-signature, and said, in exactly the voice the forms prescribed for procedural inquiry: "Warden. For the completeness of the bench's records — when a keeping under court seal is claimed against, what is the form of release from the north gallery?"

Technically permitted. A clerk may ask after forms. And morally enormous, because both of them knew the true content: there is no form. You count everything — I have watched you count for two winters — so you know to the Glimmer what sits in the north of your vault, and you know no release has ever issued, and I am asking you, across this counter, whether the thing we both serve has a door on the far side or only a mouth.

He answered with the manifest's silence. "No such claim has arisen in my tenure," he said — flawless, true, dead — and counter-signed, and issued her receipt unasked. He watched her take it with the first imprecision he had ever seen in her, a half-breath's pause, the auditor recording: answer received; answer false by omission; the warden is part of the machine. She thanked him by the form and went up the vault stairs into the light, and Telvar Thornroot stood at his counter among his perfect registers and discovered that of all the verdicts his life had served, he could not serve this one.

He came to her nine days later. Outside the vault, outside the court — he found her at the sea-wall promenade above the rebuilt harbour, in the long Twiland evening. It was the first time in two years either had seen the other beyond a counter's width, and he was carrying, wrapped in plain cloth like a man bringing bread, his private register.

"You asked after a form of release," he said, no melody in it, the formality stripped to timber. "There is none. I have waited nine days for the line, or the court, or forty years of habit, to give me a reason not to be standing here, and none has come. So. The north gallery holds the keepings of one hundred and forty estates. I have counted them since my first year; the counting is in this book; the court has never asked for it and the line does not know it exists. Drawer ninety-one holds a mariner's instruments and trade-stock, entered 1118. The same drawer holds a Moonsong token, minor branch, surrendered the same proceeding." He held the register out across the space between them, his whole bloodline's thread on his collar and his whole life in his hands. "I re-lock that drawer differently. I have re-locked it differently for fourteen years, and I never once let myself ask why it troubled me, until a clerk of the third bench started reading my counters upside down. The token's surrenderer was called Liriel. The register is yours to read, clerk. I believe some of it is yours to have."

The evening light came green through the Vine-Leaf arches of the promenade. Yrsa took the book.

It was the only love either of them ever declared — disclosure, complete, across the gap the law had built between them; an elf of the keeping line handing the kept-from the count of the keeping — and both of them understood it as it happened, with the clarity of precise people, to be that, and to be irreversible, and to be the end of every arrangement by which their two adjoining places in the world had been bearable.

<Break />

They modelled the lawful road first, because they were who they were: two clerks at a table in the human quarter, through the short summer nights, with the private register and her nine years of dockets between them, putting the court they both served on trial in its absence.

Exposure through procedure. She built the case as she had built a hundred for the bench, and they tested it joint by joint, and it failed everywhere, and failed instructively. Petition the harbour court: the petition would be heard by the bench whose seals were the evidence — the machine asked to audit its own mouth. Address the Lord: Fenrath Thornroot received commerce, not clerks; the petition would reach him as a digest prepared by the court; the digest would be the machine's account of itself. Carry it to another Lord's court: a guest-clerk and a vault-warden testifying against Thornroot keeping, in a land where a line's word outweighed a wing of documents — and Telvar's register, their single true exhibit, was itself unlawful, a warden's private count of a bloodline's private vault. Producing it destroyed its producer and tainted its content in the same hour. "The forms have no door on this side," Yrsa said at the end. "It isn't a flaw in the machine. It is the machine. The trust-rolls are the face. The north gallery is the function."

Then there was the other road, and it must be set down plainly, because the official record never carried it: she proposed it, alone, entire. And Telvar refused it for nine days.

Not from fear — she never once thought it fear — but from keeping. He was the vault. Forty years. The counting was his answer to the verdict, and what she proposed would make the count false by his own hand's omission, and he could not be the warden of an uncounted thing. It was Yrsa, court-trained, who found the form his conscience could sign. Nothing uncounted. Nothing hidden. A correction. The manifest of the Vault of the Vine, amended in a clerk's fair hand to state what the vault held and whence it came — every estate, every token, every docket-number, the whole north gallery entered at last into the light of record — and the restitution taken openly against that corrected page, item against entry, like any lawful release.

"You will not be the warden of an uncounted thing," she said. "You'll be the first warden in forty years of a counted one. For about six hours. After which they will burn the page and call it theft. But Telvar — hear the rest. They will have to choose to burn it. The machine has never once had to choose. Every wrong it has done, it has done as filing. I am going to make it commit its crime in person."

The night before, at the sea-wall, she gave him the last open door: report her intention, now, tonight, and stand clear. The court would believe a Thornroot over a guest, always, and his forty years could close in honour and a pension.

"I re-ordered my keys two winters ago," Telvar said, looking out at the dark water where the new moles stood pale on the old wave-wreck of 1115. "Because a clerk was reading my counters. I have been choosing for two winters, Yrsa. I simply lacked the form." He turned from the sea, and the green lamps caught the thread at his collar, and the smile he gave her was forty years late and entirely young. "The schedule wants correction. Let's draft it fair."

<Break />

The heist — the official record would need a word, and would choose that one — took place on the last docket-day of Reaptide, during business hours, through the front procedure, and its principal instrument was Yrsa's penmanship.

There was no breaking. There was a third-bench clerk arriving at the Vault of the Vine with the season's dockets at the prescribed hour, received at the walnut counter by the warden with the prescribed forms; and there was, among the true dockets, one folio more — drafted across nine nights in a fair hand the registrars held up as the standard of the office, sealed with the bench's own seal, which the bench's own procedure placed in the keeping of the bench's own clerks. *In the matter of the keepings of the Vault of the Vine: schedule of correction.* Page upon page: one hundred and forty estates, every drawer of the north gallery, every docket-number, every name. Corra the sail-mender's husband. The guild-marked strongboxes. The bloodline tokens, line by line. Vael, mariner. Liriel, called Vael's-wife. The unpaged ledger of nine years and the private register of forty, married into a single document and bound into the vault's official manifest, where the law's own forms required all corrections to live.

Telvar counter-signed it. Full and fair and slightly larger than required. Then he unlocked the north gallery, and the differently-locked drawer, with his own keys, by the form of release she had drafted onto the correction's final page. And the release was small — that, too, was hers, argued and fixed against all the logic of plunder: drawer ninety-one's contents, a dead mariner's sextant and instruments and a heart-wood disc with the Moonsong leaf; and one pouch of Glimmers from the forfeiture-shelves, eighty-one Glimmers, the documented cash residue of the thirty-one estates of her own inscribing. Restitution, item against entry. The rest of the gallery — a fortune that would have bought a squadron — she left counted, corrected, and untouched, because the rest was not hers, and the page was the theft that mattered.

She was at the door — past the counters, past the morning's ordinary depositors, the pouch and the wrapped instruments in a clerk's plain satchel — when the under-assessor of the second bench came down the vault stairs with a query-docket in his hand, and stopped, and looked at her satchel, and at the warden's face behind the counter, which was the face of a man standing post at his own pyre, and said: "Clerk. The bench shows no release scheduled today."

"The bench is correct," Yrsa said, in the voice of the forms, handing him her receipt — Telvar's counter-signature still drying — with the docket-number of the correction fair upon it. "It was a matter of keeping. The schedule is amended; the bench may verify against the manifest. Good day to you." And procedure — her country, her parent, her one citizenship — held the door for her one last time, because the under-assessor did what the machine had trained every soul in it to do before all else. He went to verify against the manifest.

She was at the sea-wall before the shouting started. By evening the page was burned. By midnight she was taken — at her own lodgings, at her own table, where she was waiting with the satchel unhidden, because flight would have made it theft.

<Break />

The hearing was held in the waning days of Glowfall, before the full harbour court, Lord Fenrath presiding — her matter had grown too loud for less — and the sentence, Yrsa knew with a professional's certainty, had been fixed before the first form was read. She had drafted enough of them to know the signs: the exile-writ's vellum already cut to length on the registrar's table; the escort-ship's master, paid and patient, in the gallery since morning.

The charge was glimmer-theft from a bloodline vault. Eighty-one Glimmers, documented. It was the only charge the record could carry, and she watched the court understand this before her eyes. They could not try her for taking a sextant and a token that the vault's manifest had never admitted holding — the correction was burned; to charge the restitution was to prove the page — and so the proceeding closed itself, of necessity, around the one entry that fit the lie. *A half-elf clerk stole Glimmers from her betters.* True as far as it went. The official record of Nilvelond carries it to this day, and carries nothing else, and the nothing else is the truest thing in the file.

She did not contest it. She had one thing left to protect and one coin to buy it with. The court wanted the correction's provenance — a document of that intimacy argued an accomplice with keys — and the assessors circled the question for two days, and the vault-warden sat in the gallery through all of it with his collar-thread and his ruined forty years, waiting to be named. The trade was never spoken; trades of that kind never are; it was conducted entirely in silences, hers against the court's. She declined to account for the correction's sources. Declined to account for her movements. Declined — the assessors' frustration mounting into the vaulted light — to say anything at all beyond the forms' minimum, syllable by syllable less, until the court faced its choice: convene a second proceeding against a Thornroot on inference, in public, before the trade-houses of four nations — or accept the clerk's silence, seal the file, and burn, as it were, a second page. They accepted the silence. A name traded for silence. The machine, given the choice between truth and tidiness, chose as it had always chosen; and Telvar Thornroot walked out of the gallery unnamed, un-pensioned, and un-ruined, back to the vault he would keep — counted, always counted — for years unremarked.

Lord Fenrath pronounced the exile himself, pragmatic and brief: removal by water, return upon pain of the law, the court's mercy noted in the records. He looked at her once, with the cosmopolitan curiosity of a man assessing a discrepancy his clerks had failed to explain, and Yrsa looked back at the wealthiest of the Elven Lords and said nothing whatsoever; and watched something pass over the commerce-lord's face — not guilt; the machine had no organ for it; perhaps only arithmetic, as if a column somewhere had moved that he could not locate. *Keep your ledgers well, my lord*, she thought, in the unpaged book, in the place where her voice now lived. *We will speak again in them.*

They put her on the escort-ship at the morning tide. The clerks of the third bench did not come to the mole; one understands. The warden did not come; that, too, had been drafted between them at the sea-wall, fair, like everything they made — no farewell on the record; the record has taken enough. She stood at the stern in the plain clothes of the struck-off, with a satchel holding a sextant, a heart-wood disc, and the eighty-one Glimmers the court — procedure to the last — had been obliged to return to her on the harbour-master's scales — restitution being unprovable, and the coin, by the receipt the court itself had sealed into the closed file, hers. And she watched Nilvelond go down into the sea: the Vine-Leaf terraces, the rebuilt moles, the green and silver city that had taught her forms, taken her parents, and made her, in its courteous machinery, twice — once a clerk, and once this.

The escort-master, making conversation on the tenth day out, asked her name for his log. She considered the question longer than it wanted. The clerk's name was struck. The line's name had been surrendered in a drawer fourteen years before she took it back.

"Yrsa," she said, and nothing else; and the silence after it sat so finally in the air that the master wrote, in the log of that crossing, the first attested instance of the name the Serene Sea would come to know in every harbour-court ledger and every Thornroot manifest from Nilvelond to the Reach: *Yrsa, called the Quiet.*

A season later, off the Twiland coast, a hired ship with a pale, salt-bleached mainsail overhauled a Thornroot-flagged trader out of Nilvelond and took from her — with courteous, documented precision, against a schedule the boarders carried in a fair clerk's hand — her manifested magical goods, and nothing else, and harmed no one. An envoy of the Crimson Tide, hearing of it, came months after with compliments and an offer of code-terms under the Articles. The pale ship's captain heard him out in total silence and sent back four words, which entered the lore of two fleets and explained, to anyone who could read an entry, everything that would ever lie between Yrsa the Quiet and Dargan Kelvar's lawful, chartered, well-kept sea:

*"Codes are courts, floating."*

The unspoken column in Lord Fenrath's ledgers begins that year. It has not closed since.
