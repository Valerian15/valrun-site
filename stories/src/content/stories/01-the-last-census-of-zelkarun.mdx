The forty-first name on the census roll was a child of six, and Velanne Tharalyn could not see why the family wanted her counted.

"Born abroad," she said, reading the entry back to the wet-nurse who had brought the girl to the harbour office. "Both parents citizens of Zelkarun. Resident at Greymouth these four years." The girl was drawing in the dust of the floor with one finger — a spiral, the sea's great current, the way children drew it without being taught. "She has never set foot on the island. Why does the island need her name?"

"Because the instruction says all of them," the wet-nurse said. "You wrote the instruction, mistress. You'd know better than me."

Velanne had not written the instruction. It had come in the spring under the seal of her great-uncle's office in the white city across the water: a full accounting of every citizen of Zelkarun abroad — name, age, trade, household, dependents; children entered without exception; the rolls copied fair and lodged with the station's record-keeper. It had landed on the desk of the most junior clerk of the most minor station on the southern coast of Verdure, which was to say on hers.

She entered the child. After the child she entered a glassblower of fifty-two, a pilot's widow, three apprentice shipwrights, and a scholar of tides who had not seen Zelkarun in thirty years and wept a little when his name went down. Between the scholar and the noon watch she took out the other paper, the one that lived under the lid of her writing-box, and added a sentence to it.

The paper was a petition for recall. It asked, in the third courteous formulation she had attempted that season, that the clerk Velanne Tharalyn be returned from Greymouth station to the island, to duties suited to her training; that two years of counting fishermen at the edge of the world be deemed service enough. She had been promised the diplomatic halls of the white city. She had been sent to a town that smelled of pitch and herring, to keep lists.

Every army must have bread before it has banners, the harbourmaster had told her once, when she complained in his hearing. She had taken it for mockery. It was the kindest thing anyone said to her that year.

She was reaching for the sand-shaker when the light changed.

It brightened. At midday, with no cloud moving, the room grew lighter, and the window threw a second shadow of the casement across her desk, and both shadows leaned. Out on the quay a man shouted. Then many men. Then nobody, which was worse.

She went out onto the steps with the census roll still in her hand. The whole town was looking south. Far out, past the last fishing grounds, a column of light stood up from the sea into the roof of the heavens, white at the root, and at its head a bloom of fire opened with the terrible patience of very distant things.

There was no sound. The sound was still at sea, coming toward them across a hundred leagues of water.

She thought of the petition lying open on her desk, the ink of its newest sentence not yet sanded. *Duties suited to her training.* The fire in the south widened. A wind came off the water and passed through the town.

She went back inside. She set a paperweight on the petition. She carried the census roll to the strong-chest and locked it in, and stood with her back against the chest as the first sound arrived — not a crack but a pressure, felt in the chest-bone and the harbour glass.

Standing there, she thought of the instruction. It had been the fourth of its kind in two years. Each more exacting than the last. Children entered without exception. She did not know a single thing about why she was here.

The keeper of records at Greymouth station had no name the harbour could pronounce, so the harbour had translated it: Counts-the-Ships. The name was accurate. Counting was the duty; the ships were what Greymouth offered to be counted.

When the sky in the south caught fire, Counts-the-Ships was on the mole with a smooth grey stone held in both hands, taking the noon record: eleven hulls at anchor, four at the quay, one inbound with a torn foresail. The Sketh did not put down the stone. The duty did not say record the harbour except when the sky burns. The duty said record.

So the stone received the burning. The column of light, its measured widths against the horizon. The bloom at its head, and the colours in it, which were not the colours of any hearth-fire. The doubled shadows of the mooring posts. The inbound ship coming about, wallowing, coming about again, her crew's faces all turned one way. The stone received the silence of the gulls. Counts-the-Ships judged this the most significant fact of the morning and held it longest. Gulls were never silent over Greymouth. Some Sketh, some day, would touch this stone and need to know that.

Sound arrived. The harbour folk cried out, and some knelt. Counts-the-Ships stood and received the sound too, and after it the human noise, which was part of the record whether the humans wished it kept or not.

Within the watch the rumours began, and the keeper recorded those as well, each in its time, without weighing them: that the island had signalled for aid; that the island had sent the fire up itself as a beacon; that a fleet was coming; that nothing was coming. A woman of the fish-quay said the sea had felt wrong all spring — the set of the current strange, the deep lines coming up sour — and that she had said so, and no one had written it down. This was true. It went into the stone.

In the late watch the keeper climbed to the record-room above the harbour office, where the station's stones lay ranked in their wooden cradles — nine hundred years of partnership between the Sketh and the people of the island, stone by stone, station by station. Counts-the-Ships set the day's stone in its place, rested both hands on it, and held, longer than the duty required, the one fact not yet spoken aloud anywhere in Greymouth.

The fire stood exactly where the island was.

<Break />

No word came north for nineteen days.

No packet-boat, no merchantman out of the white city's roads, no signal passed up the coast. The fishermen who worked the southern grounds came back early, saying the current had shifted out of all memory; twice in that span the tide stood a hand's breadth shy of its mark and no one could say why. Ash began to arrive on the southerly winds, a fine grey dust that gathered in the seams of sails and on windowsills and turned the sunsets to furnace colours. The harbour folk swept it from their doorsteps each morning and did not speak of it.

Velanne worked. It was that or stand on the mole staring south with the rest, and her family did not stand and stare; the discovery that this was bred into her deeper than grief came as its own small ugliness. She drew up lists of every Zelkarun-flagged hull in port and every citizen the census named, because if survivors came north someone would have to feed and house and answer for them, and the only instrument in Greymouth equal to that was her own roll.

On the fifth night, copying by lamplight, she finally read the thing she had spent two years writing.

Not the entries — the shape. The census was too new and too complete. No accounting of citizens abroad needed renewal four times in two years unless what it tracked was being moved. And it was weighted: toward the children, and toward trades listed not by guild custom but by usefulness — shipwrights, healers, smiths, scholars of tides and stars. Households noted with their stores and their sailing stock. Page by page, the roll stopped being a list of the island's people abroad and became an inventory of what a civilization would need if it expected, quite soon, to consist only of what was abroad.

She sat still for a long time, the lamp burning down, the ash ticking against the window.

Her posting. Itharen posted to the Twiland coast the same spring. Maren sent north to the river courts two years gone. Half a generation of Tharalyn juniors dealt outward across the rim of the sea. Postings suited, they had all been told, to their training.

She thought of the prophets. Everyone on the island had known of them — the criers of correction, dismissed from the colonnades, dismissed at her own family's table. She had heard her great-uncle do it himself, with the dry completeness for which the Tharalyn were hired by half the courts of the world. *The Great Work is measured, child. The current is watched. The matter is closed.*

The matter is closed. And the same season, under his seal, the fourth instruction: count the children. Count all of them.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the empty room. The words were not grief yet. They were arithmetic. Whatever had happened in the south — and she already knew the size of it, in the place below argument — her family had been founding the survival of it for years, in her hand, without once telling her what she carried.

At first light she went down to the harbour to learn what the wrong-running sea would bring her. The keeper of records was already on the mole, stone in hand, facing south.

<Break />

To whom is a record owed when its principal is dead?

The question had been arriving for nineteen days. Counts-the-Ships turned it over while recording the grain-sloops and the wrong tide. The partnership was nine hundred years old: the Sketh kept the records, the island was the records' home, and one day each stone went south in a Zelkarun hull to lie in the libraries of the white city. That was the shape of the duty. The shape assumed the white city.

The harbour, meanwhile, had begun to look sideways at foreigners.

It was small at first. A fish-wife's blessing left unsaid. A boy called indoors when the keeper passed. Then the grain-factor refused the station's note of hand, politely, twice, and the harbourmaster's man came to ask whether the record-room's stones could be read by anyone — by the harbour court, for instance, times being uncertain, the town having a right to know what was kept about it. Counts-the-Ships answered in the only language the duty permitted: the extreme-slowdown gesture, supplemented with posture. The long refusal, made gently. The harbourmaster's man went away calling it insolence. The town's word for the Sketh that autumn became the lizard that watches, and the children took up throwing pebbles, and all of it went into the stones, because the duty did not say record the town except when the town turns.

The keeper understood the town. To record is to understand, whether or not understanding is wanted. The town had fed on the island's trade for nine generations; the trade was a column of fire now; fear must go somewhere, and foreigners are where fear goes. Every army must have bread before it has banners, the harbour folk said, meaning: do not ask us to be better than our stores allow.

On the twentieth day the first survivor ship passed the heads.

She was a Zelkarun packet, deep-laden, her paint scorched along the larboard strake, her people at the rails in the stillness of those who have used up every other way of standing. Counts-the-Ships recorded her name, her line, her draught — and then, breaking the order of every noon record made in nine hundred years, recorded her people first and her hull after. Who stood. Who could not. A child held up at the rail to see the strange green coast that was now the only kind of land there was.

That evening the keeper lifted the day-stone and understood that the question had finished arriving. To whom is a record owed when its principal is dead? To the principal's return. The records were no longer the island's library abroad. They were the island. The keeping was now guarding; and after guarding, when guarding too had outlived its makers, it would be waiting — however long — for the ones who could read to come back and claim what had been held.

The Tharalyn clerk was below, lamplit, working her roll of names against the packet's manifest. Counts-the-Ships watched her for the length of three breaths, which for a Sketh is a long attention, and set the thought into the stone in the old tongue of the island:

*Lyrenes in marun.* Memory in stone.

What else outlives a city?

The *Gull of Morning* was a coastal trader of no great draught, and her master wanted triple the peace-time rate to take a Tharalyn clerk south into water he called disarranged. Velanne paid it out of the station's chest and entered the sum in the ledger under *necessary*, which was the truest word she wrote that year.

They went south five days through a sea that had forgotten its manners. The set of the current was a half-point wrong, then a full point. The master swore at his charts, then stopped consulting them, then stopped swearing, which in him was a mark of fear. Ash-fall came on the second day; the crew tied cloths over their mouths; with the ash came a smell the sea had no right to carry — burnt stone, and under it something sweeter and worse. On the third night the lesser moon rose discoloured through the haze, and the helmsman, a Verdure man of sixty, said, "When the small lamp burns red, mend your nets at home," and held his course anyway, because triple rate is triple rate.

Velanne stood her own watches at the rail, unasked. The looking steadied her. She had been raised to read rooms, and the sea off Cinder — the name was already moving through the fleet; no one could say who had first said it — told her plainly that the world had been rearranged by something that had not consulted it.

They raised the gathering on the sixth morning.

Ships — forty, sixty, the count kept failing as the haze opened and closed — lying hove-to in loose company on a slate-grey swell. A nation of perhaps three thousand souls riding at sea anchor, because the land they kept station on could no longer be landed on. And beyond them, southward, where the green island should have stood up with its terraces and its towers: a low black country of smoke, lying on the sea from rim to rim, and rising out of it, where the wind worked the haze, the heads of dark spires that no hand had built — still growing, like the fingers of something thrown down so hard the world had closed over it and was now, slowly, gripping back.

The crew stopped working. The master took the cloth from his mouth as if at a graveside.

Velanne found she was holding the census roll against her chest with both arms, and had been for some time.

"Where away?" the master asked her, hoarse. Of all these hulls, which do I hail?

She searched the ships for the flag of her family's office, and found it three times — three hulls flying the courier's blue of the Tharalyn diplomatic line, riding together at the heart of the gathering.

"There," she said. "The blue. My family will be organising something."

She had meant it bitterly. It came out as the simple truth.

Her cousin Itharen had been posted to the Twiland coast the same spring she was sent to Greymouth. The last time she had seen him, he had been complaining about it across their grandmother's table with his mouth full. She found him aboard the flagship of the blue, behind a trestle on the maindeck, with a queue of survivors before him and a roll of names under his hand.

The roll was the census. Her census — the same ruled columns, the same ordering, children entered without exception — in his hand, under his seal, with the Twiland coast's names where hers had Verdure's.

He looked up and saw her and did not look surprised. That was the moment the thing went all the way in. Not the fire, not the smoke-country to the south. A cousin's unsurprised face, a twin roll on a trestle, and the knowledge that her two years of resentment had been one tooth of an instrument the size of the sea.

They got the queue seen to before they spoke. That, too, was bred in.

"How long have you known?" she asked him, at the rail, in the dark.

"Known?" Itharen was thinner; the table-wit had been burned off him, and underneath was their grandmother's face. "Velanne, I knew when you knew. Nineteen days ago, with everyone else."

"The census—"

"Was a census. I cursed mine the way I expect you cursed yours. The instruction to open the sealed annex came after, by signal, from the senior envoy. Aunt Sera's office had lodged one at every station. *In the event of the loss of the island.* Stores, sailing stock, the muster of trades, the order in which things are to be done. The annex is dated four years ago. The earliest draft in it is dated eleven."

Eleven years. She had been a girl of eleven, learning the diplomatic hand by copying treaty-forms at her great-uncle's elbow, while at that same elbow, in that same hand, someone had begun drafting the survival of their civilization.

"They mocked the prophets," she said. "I heard it. At our own table, Itharen. *The matter is closed.*"

"At table," said a third voice, "where the household servants could hear and repeat, the matter was closed." The senior surviving envoy of the Tharalyn line had come up the deck unannounced — a small upright woman of sixty, an aunt of the third degree, ash worked so deep into the lines of her face that grief and dust could no longer be told apart. "In the annexes, the matter was provided for. Both were policy, and the second could not have survived the first being dropped. If the family had been seen to believe the prophets, child, what do you imagine the island would have done with that?"

"They might have *lived*—"

"They might have torn the works apart in a season of riot, against a correction most of them did not believe in — and then it comes, or it does not, and either way the Tharalyn name is spent, the rolls were never made, the children were never moved." She said it without heat, in the dry completeness of the family's trade, and Velanne heard underneath it, for the first time in her life, what the dryness was for. "We could not save the island. We never once believed we could. We could count what could not be kept, and move what could. You were what could."

The sea worked against the hull. To the south, the smoke-country showed a dull red seam where it met the water.

"You should have told us," Velanne said at last. "Not the island. Us. The ones you sent."

"Yes," the envoy said, and the admission was worse than any defence, because it closed nothing. "And we judged that twenty postings would be borne better than twenty goodbyes that could not be explained. We were wrong about you, or right; you will decide which over the rest of your life. Your mother entered your name on the first roll herself. If you want her goodbye, Velanne, it is there. It is a ledger line. It was the only language we permitted ourselves."

Everyone she had not said goodbye to had been said goodbye to for her, in ruled columns, years before the sky burned. She had carried the goodbyes from house to house in Greymouth and entered them in her best hand and resented every one.

She did not weep then. She wept later, at sea, on the first night of the journey north; and what she wept for, as much as the dead, was the petition under the paperweight on her desk — the small furious paper of a woman who had wanted, above everything, to be recalled to the island in time to die there.

In the spring of the first year, the keepers began coming home — except that home now meant a direction, not a place.

They came by ones and twos: the Sketh of the stations, out of the river courts and the high coast and the far side of the sea, each carrying or convoying the stones of their keeping, moving south toward the ruin none of them could enter. Counts-the-Ships received three of them at Greymouth that year, recorded their stones' arrival, and stood with them in the record-room in the silence the harbour mistook for coldness. It was a conversation. It was too slow for the harbour to hear.

What passed in it, stone by stone, was the new shape of the duty.

The island's libraries were gone, and with them the readers. The tongue of the partnership now lived in a handful of scattered survivors and in the stones themselves, and each year of dying would make it smaller. Some keeper in the river courts had proposed that the records be opened — read into Common, given to the harbour courts, made useful. The answer that moved back along the chain was the long refusal, made gently. A record opened to those who cannot read it is not opened; it is spent. The duty had been: keep, and deliver south. The duty now was: keep, and guard, and wait.

Wait for whom? The stones did not say, because the stones did not know. The keepers held only this: the gift of the work had come from the island; the island's people were scattered and few but not ended; a thing nine hundred years in the keeping does not change hands in a panic. They would hold the ruins and the stones until the ones who could read returned. They would know them when they saw them. If the waiting outlived the keepers, the keepers' children would wait, and theirs, and the waiting itself would become the duty.

On the last day of the year, Counts-the-Ships took the noon record from the mole as always: nine hulls at anchor, two at the quay, the tide a hand's breadth wrong, gulls loud. The name the harbour had given was no longer accurate; there was no Zelkarun left to flag a ship. The keeper considered whether a truer name was required, and decided against. Let the name stand and become its own record — of a time when there had been ships worth a duty's counting.

The stone received the gulls. Somewhere south, under the smoke, the broken engine of the old world went on doing whatever it now did, unwitnessed. That was the one record no keeper would hold; and Counts-the-Ships, who understood records, understood what it means when the largest fact in the world goes unrecorded. It means the fact is not finished.

In the fifth year after the Impact they swore the compact on the southern shore of Cinder, in the shadow of the Bastion's unfinished walls, and Velanne Tharalyn wrote it down, because by then the writing-down of things had become what she was for.

The fortress stood half-made above the gulf, black, immense, its courses cut from the glassy stone of the spires themselves; the Zelkaris builders had decreed from the first that the wound's own substance should watch the wound. Below it on the strand, under an ash-pale sky and the eyes of perhaps a thousand survivors, the lords of the coasts came to swear: three from the southern shores of Verdure, salt-cloaked, plain-spoken men of harbour towns whose names would never trouble a chronicle; two from Twiland's green margin; and across from them the envoy of the Tharalyn, small and upright still, with five years of the work cut into her face.

There were no thrones present. There was no crown anywhere in the world, that year, worth the name. There were coast-lords with grain and hulls, a family with nothing left but its word, and between them a fortress built to stand watch over a buried fire forever.

Velanne read the sworn words aloud for the parties, and again slowly for the clerks. Her own hand had drafted them through eleven nights of argument: that the lords of the southern coasts would victual and man and succour the Black Bastion in every year and every weather; that the Bastion would stand watch over the wound of the world on behalf of all the living; that the watch would not lapse though kingdoms lapsed; and that the parties swore not as sovereigns, who die, but as the coasts to the last fortress of the living — words she had defended against three drafting clerks who wanted something with more dignity and less truth.

The lords made their marks. The envoy signed for the family. The wind came off the gulf and worried the parchment. Far inland, above the haze, the heads of the Onyx Spires stood black and complete against the brown noon — finished, or seeming finished, the way held things seem.

Afterward, when the boats had gone out and the strand was empty, Velanne sat on a course of black stone with her writing-box on her knees and did not open it for a long while.

She had gone back to Greymouth, that first winter, to close out the station. The office swept, the rolls in order, the record-room emptied of its stones — and on her desk the paperweight, where she had left it, holding down her petition for recall. She read the petition through once, standing, in her travelling cloak. Then she filed it. Not burned it: filed it, under its date, in the station's permanent record, where some archivist of a future age would find one junior clerk's furious, courteous, unsent demand to be returned to the island in time for the end of the world. It was a true record of what it had cost. The family had its annexes. Let the archive have her.

Now, on the strand below the Bastion, she opened the writing-box and took out a fresh quire, and at the head of the first leaf wrote the only title she had ever been sure of:

*An accounting of the people of Zelkarun abroad in the last years of the island, set down by one who carried the rolls and did not know what she carried.*

She would be years at it. She would tell the truth, which the official record would never do; the family's record gives postings and dates, and the matter is closed. The truth was this. The island had been warned and had laughed. Some who laughed in public obeyed in secret, and were right — and being right has a taste no one should be asked to praise. Nothing, no instrument, no foresight, no discipline of any family, can hold what is not meant to be held. But what is let go in time, in good order, with its name entered fair in a ruled column, may yet be found floating after the wave has passed.

The dead had no marker but a country of black glass. The living had lists.

She thought, briefly, of the reader. Someone would shelve this, someday, in the black fortress above her; someone not yet born would pull it down for reasons of their own. She found she could write to that person. After two years of census-taking, it was the one recipient she was sure existed.

She squared the leaf, dipped her pen, and began with a child of six, born abroad, who had drawn the sea's great spiral in the dust of a harbour office floor on the last morning of the old world.
