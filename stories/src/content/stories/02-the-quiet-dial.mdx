The dial that governed the throttle-gate had not moved in eleven years, and this, the senior calibration officer told the three committee members who had come to look at it, was entirely expected.

"It's set at safe-hold," she said. "The gate is at three-sixths. All gauges nominal. You can read the board yourselves."

The three read the board. Two of them read it with the satisfied expressions of men who are about to write a report they have already outlined. The third — the young assessor from the Covenant office, the one who had been asking inconvenient questions since the review began — read it longer.

"Residue variance on the deep-tap lines," he said. "Channels seven through eleven. That's a four-point spread over the baseline we were given."

"Calibration drift," she said. "It's within the maintenance band."

"The maintenance band in the standing report," he said, "is based on a baseline that's eleven years old." He looked up at her, not with hostility — the assessor's way was careful, almost apologetic — "When was the baseline last measured?"

There was a pause of the kind she had become expert in: not ignorance, not deception, but the moment before a person decided which accurate thing to say.

"The baseline is validated at each full-cycle review," she said. "The last full-cycle review was eleven years ago."

Her name was Tayne Olvari, and she had accepted this post against her own judgment, which she kept as a separate record from her professional one.

The professional record said: the Aetheric Anchor, Zelkarun, was the largest thaumaturgical engine in the world; the maintenance of its calibration was the most consequential technical post on the island; the senior calibration officer was paid accordingly and commanded a team of twelve and the respect of the Covenant's engineering corps. The personal record said: the engine had been throttled eleven years ago following a series of resonance events whose cause was never formally established; the position of senior calibration officer had, in those eleven years, seen four holders; and she was the first who had previously worked at the deep-tap interface rather than above it.

She knew what the four-point spread on channels seven through eleven meant, because she had spent six years learning the language of the lines before she was promoted to a desk that looked at them from the outside. She had been writing that meaning in the reports for two years with careful, defensible precision — the words of a technical specialist, not an alarmist — and watching the words arrive in the committee's correspondence as validation of an engine running within its maintenance band.

She had, in those two years, considered and dismissed the obvious interpretation: that the committee was corrupt, that the finding was being suppressed for institutional reasons, that someone in the Covenant's senior structure had decided the Anchor would run at three-sixths until it couldn't. It was neater than the truth. The truth was that her reports were accurate. The maintenance band was correctly calculated from the baseline. The drift was within it. Every statement in the professional record was defensible.

The baseline was eleven years old and had been taken before the throttle event, and no one, in eleven years, had taken another one because the engine was running within its band, and you did not disturb an engine running within its band.

What the deep-tap lines were saying, in the language she had spent six years learning, was: the band is wrong. The question was whether that was a technical judgment or a political one, and whether the difference mattered to the engine.

The assessor came back alone in the fourth watch, which was neither the strangest thing that had happened in the calibration room nor the most welcome.

"The door was open," he said, which was technically true; she had not locked it because she was still in it.

"I know what you want," she said, without looking up from the variance chart she was updating.

"I want the raw data from the deep-tap channels for the last two years." He paused. "Please."

"You can request it through the committee. I'll have it to the secretary's office by the end of the week."

"I'm requesting it from you, here, tonight." Another pause. "I've read your reports. All of them. I'm not your enemy in this, Calibration Officer."

She looked up then. The assessor — Meravel, his name was; she had finally learned it on the third day of the review — was young enough that the late watch showed in his face, and tired enough not to be performing anything. She considered whether a person with his access to the committee's correspondence could be using this language without knowing what it cost.

"The raw data doesn't change the reports," she said. "The drift is within the maintenance band. That's a fact."

"The maintenance band is calculated from an eleven-year-old baseline," Meravel said. "Also a fact."

"One that appears in every report I have filed."

"Where it reads as a disclosure," he said, "rather than a finding. You know the difference."

She did. The silence between them was the silence of two people who had identified the same problem and arrived at different points about what to do with it. She went back to the chart for a moment, then set down her pen.

"If I give you the raw data," she said, "and you take it to the committee, there are three outcomes. First: the committee orders a full recalibration, which means halting the engine, which the Covenant has refused twice on grounds of disruption to island function. Second: the committee finds the data inconclusive and the review closes with the maintenance band endorsed, and I have been shown to be one of those technical officers who gives raw data to assessors informally at the fourth watch, which shortens my career and my influence from this post. Third: something that is neither of those."

"Third," Meravel said quietly.

"Third requires me to know where you're taking it before I give it to you."

He told her. It was not the committee. It was not an official channel of any kind. It was a man she had heard of — a prophet of the correction, one of the criers, someone whose name had begun appearing in places where official channels had been tried and found willing — a man who had spent eight years assembling the technical case for the engine's danger from publicly available records and was two documents short of making it impossible to dismiss. Two documents.

She knew, sitting there, what the professional record would say: this is not a defensible act. She knew what the personal record said, because she had been keeping it for two years and it said the same thing it said now. She went to the secondary cabinet, unlocked it with her own key, and copied the raw data with her own hand, in figures not reports, because figures can only be argued with by other figures.

"The baseline question," she said, copying, "is the fulcrum. If the baseline has moved — and the deep-tap pattern suggests it has moved by a margin that would put the actual drift outside any defensible maintenance band — then the engine is not running within specification. It is running within a measurement that stopped measuring the right thing eleven years ago." She blotted the final column. "That is my technical judgment. I am not putting it in a report because reports have a recipient. This has one. Give it to him."

The shrine was minor and the coastal road was empty at the turn of the tide, and Tayne Olvari was not sure why she had come here except that the engine room had been making her dreams architectural, and the open air helped.

The shrine was to one of the southern sea-figures, older than the Covenant and therefore acceptable to everyone, which was its function: a rough-carved column of black stone, a dish for offerings, a view of the water with no obstruction. She sat on the step below the column and watched the sea because the sea, at this latitude, was the visible output of the thing she spent her working hours managing from the inside. The current ran off the Anchor's southern vents; everyone knew this; the current had always run this way; the fishermen reckoned by it. If the engine changed — when the engine changed, she corrected herself, which was a choice she noticed making — the current would be the first visible thing to move.

She had given the data to Meravel four nights ago and had no idea what had happened to it. The professional record had added an entry: *data transferred to Assessor Meravel, informal; copy retained in personal files.* The personal record had added nothing, because the personal record was a judgment and the judgment was already made.

The sea was doing what the sea did, which was everything at once. She watched the set of the current — unchanged, nominal, the way it had been for eleven years since the engine went to three-sixths — and thought about baselines.

Eleven years ago someone had measured the deep-tap channels and called the result a baseline. The measurement was honest. The result was accurate. The engine was running at that level, and this was the level at which the engine ran, and a thing running at its level is running at its level. This was defensible. This was, she was nearly sure, exactly the reasoning that had produced the report the committee kept finding satisfying.

A baseline that has not been re-measured is not a baseline. It is a fixed point in time that the world continues moving away from, and the distance between the fixed point and the world accumulates silently in every instrument that reads against it. The drift is within the band. The band is drawn around the old point. The world is somewhere else.

She had known this for two years. The reason she had filed disclosures rather than findings was sitting here with her on the shrine step, in the cool air off the water: a finding required a prescription. A disclosure required only accuracy. A finding, filed formally through the right channels, would produce one of two outcomes — the engine halted, or the finding overruled — and in either case the calibration officer who had filed it would spend the rest of her career being one of two things: vindicated or wrong. Either way she would be known for the finding, and the finding would become the story, and the story was a blunt instrument that did not fit the delicate machinery of what she actually knew.

She had been wrong about this. Not about the machinery — the machinery was what it was — but about the story. A disclosure filed carefully in a report whose recipient was the committee was not precision. It was privacy. She had been keeping her real judgment in the personal record, which no one could compel, where it was safe and useless, and submitting the professional record, where it looked like honesty but functioned as nothing.

The water was grey and moving and indifferent. The current ran the same way it had run for eleven years. She thought of the man Meravel's data had gone to, and hoped he was the kind of person whose story was not too blunt.

She rose, left a coin in the dish for the column's sea-figure, and walked back up the road toward the city, and the engine, and whatever the measurement of the world, which would not hold still.

The report that came back to the calibration room from the committee at the close of the review endorsed the maintenance band and noted the eleven-year-old baseline as a matter for the next scheduled full-cycle review, currently due in two years.

Tayne Olvari read it once, filed it, and began drafting a request — not to the committee; to the Covenant's chief surveyor, through the proper technical channel, with the full professional record as annex — for authorization to take a new baseline measurement from the deep-tap interface.

The request was accurate. The language was a finding's language. The recipient was someone who could act on it.

She did not know if Meravel's prophet had found use for the figures. She did not know — would not know, until much later, in the form of other people's accounts of a sky on fire — whether the baseline, if taken, would have confirmed the drift or corrected it, or whether the correction would have come in time to matter. These questions, she filed in the personal record, where they remain, under the heading she had given the section in the second year: *things the professional record cannot hold, being too heavy for paper and too important to burn.*

The request for a new baseline went up the proper channel and was received and acknowledged. The acknowledgement did not include a date.
