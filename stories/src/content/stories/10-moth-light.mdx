The brood of Reaptide came due in the first week of Glowfall, and Theliar of the warden-line of Velwyn sat at the brood-ledger in the lantern-loft of the World Tree, doing what eleven generations of his family had done before him: writing down how long the imprinted lives had lasted.

Six days. Six. Six. Five and the better part of a sixth — he entered it as six, by the rounding rule his grandmother had taught him — and his pen stopped over the column.

The column was wrong. Not any single entry; each had its release-mark and its return-mark and its witnessed fading, all in order. The column. Whisper moths lived their unimprinted spans like any small silk-winged thing; it was the imprinting that crowned them — the spoken message, the recipient's signature pressed into the Pattern of them — and an imprinted moth of the World Tree broods lived seven days. Had lived seven days in his grandmother's ledgers, and her grandmother's. He checked, that night, going back through the loft's shelf of bound years with the care of a man counting his own pulse. Sevens, regular as leaf-fall, running back centuries. A scatter of sixes-and-sevens beginning about a decade past. Then, brood by brood through 1145 and 1146, the sevens going out of the book, until this season — Reaptide of 1146 — the first brood in the recorded history of the office to return no seven at all.

He did what wardens did: he suspected the stock. Thin breeding, a weak season, the silk-lines wanting outcross. The guild's own proverb said it: blame the brood before the world. He pulled the breeding rolls and found them sound. He weighed the season and found it mild. He sat in the loft with the lamps low and the Tree's slow silver light coming in at the window, and looked from the ledger to the light and back, and refused, with a craftsman's whole strength, the thought that had come to sit beside him.

It stayed. It had been sitting beside the ledgers for ten years, he would understand later, waiting for one warden to round one entry down.

The imprint draws on the ground the Tree stands in. Every apprentice learned it in the first year: the message lives on the moth as the glowvine lives on the Tree, and the Tree on the deep current under everything. Stock sound. Season mild.

What if it's the ground?

He closed the ledger. He was twenty-nine years old, eleventh of his line, and he possessed one column of sixes and no one in the world he could say that sentence to.

From the high gallery of the Dawn Bower, Aerlys Aethyn heard the morning rite fail, and counted, while her heart did something slower and colder, exactly where.

The rite was the Lifting of the Leaves, sung at first light by rotation. This morning's voice was Sylenne of the Loranthus line, third-century, the finest young instrument among her forty — a voice Aerlys had marked for Songcaster training two years past. The rite rose as it had risen for two thousand years, and at the third sustaining — the long held Pattern-note where the song stops being beautiful and starts being work, where the singer's core takes up the true draw — the voice frayed. Not broke: frayed, at the edges; recovered; carried the rite home by craft and courage. Only three ears in Luneberg could have told that anything had happened, and two of them belonged to the singer.

The third sat in the gallery with her carved-bone flute across her knees, conducting two audits at once.

The first audit was her office. Chief Songcaster: forty singers, the rites, the rotations, and now a decision on her desk whether she summoned it or not. This was the fourth fraying since spring. Always Sylenne. Always the third sustaining. And the Bower's doctrine on the matter was as old as the Bower and as gentle as a blade: a beautiful voice is a measure of spiritual depth. A failing voice, therefore, would be read — was already being read, in the under-galleries — as a failing in the singer. Distraction. Unworthiness. The dimming of a line's blood. There was a young moth-warden, the gossip added, with whom the Loranthus girl had been seen walking the waymarkers; and the gossip found that this explained everything, and was satisfied.

The second audit she conducted as she conducted it every morning, in the silence under her own stillness, and told no one, because it was not hers to tell. She listened to the third sustaining itself. To the place where the song met the ground. Her father had walked beside her every evening for ten years carrying something he would not fully hand her — she knew its shape, as a daughter knows: the Aetherflow weakening, sensed at the strongest nexus in the world by the one lord most finely tuned to it — and every morning of those ten years Aerlys had reached down through the rite with her trained senses, wanting to feel the floor tilt. To confirm him. To make him not alone.

She felt nothing. The current came up through the Bower full and sweet as ever. Her father grew quieter every season. And now the best young voice in Twiland was fraying at precisely the note that cost the most ground; and Aerlys Aethyn, poised between a doctrine that would blame the girl and a dread that would indict the world, did what she did best and despised most in herself.

She underspoke. "Again tomorrow," she said to the rotation-keeper, mildly, rising. "The same rite. The same voice."

An instrument under test does not know it is under test. She went down the gallery stair composing nothing.

The Singer came to the lantern-loft on a wet evening in Glowfall, hooded, with a Glimmer in her palm and a request the office heard a dozen times a season: a private moth. One message, one recipient, no entry in the public rolls beyond the fee.

Theliar knew her — Luneberg was a city the size of a rumour, and the Loranthus collar-thread was not easily mistaken — but the office's discretion was older than curiosity, so he prepared the imprinting as for a stranger: the chosen moth in the release-frame, the signature-focus, the speaking-horn of waxed silk. "Whenever you are ready, my lady."

What she spoke into the horn was not a letter. It was a practice-passage — sixteen rising phrases of the Lifting of the Leaves, sung soft and close into the silk, addressed (the signature told him as he worked, and he forgot it the same instant, by the office's discipline) to her own line-mother in the southern groves. A daughter sending home the proof of her voice. Singers did it before examinations, before betrothals; mothers judged what masters judged, and a moth carried the voice true.

The moth took the imprint, woke into its crowned life, and died on the loft's sill four days later, mid-return, its message half-faded. Theliar, who had released it himself and logged its release himself, stood over the small grey body that midnight with his ledger open, and entered the first four in the history of the warden-line of Velwyn.

He went to her. It was improper by every measure — the office did not call on clients; a warden did not knock at the Singers' houses — and he went because he could not bear the bookkeeping alone another week. When she opened the door he held up the dead moth in its folded leaf, like a man returning something broken, and said, with no rank and no preamble: "My lady. Your moth failed. I have to tell you that it isn't the first, and it wasn't your message, and it wasn't my stock — and I can't tell you what it was, because I don't know, and I am the person in this city whose family has known for eleven generations."

Sylenne of Loranthus looked at the moth for a long time. Then she looked at him with the eyes of someone hearing her own secret pronounced in a stranger's mouth, and stood back from the door.

"Come in, warden," she said. "I'm going to sing you something. And then you're going to tell me, on your office, whether your moths and my voice are dying of the same thing."

<Break />

She sang him the third sustaining in her bare practice-room with the glowvine turned low, and stopped it herself at the fray, precisely, like a fencer pulling a touch, and said: "There. You heard it. Now — what it costs."

And then she taught him, across that evening and the weeks after, what no warden's ledger held: the economy of a voice. That the rite's ornaments were cheap and its sustainings dear. That the third sustaining was the costliest draw in the morning canon, the note where the singer stops spending breath and starts spending ground. That the failures — she had charted them, of course she had charted them; she was Loranthus; her family had been charting things since the Empire — came only there, and at the rite's equivalents, the long Pattern-holds. Never at speed. Never at height. Never anywhere mere skill could falter. "I sang the spring examinations flawlessly," she said, flat, dry-eyed. "Two hours. Then I failed a held ninth that a second-century child sings at festivals. Skill doesn't fail like that. Wells fail like that — at the deep bucket, in a dry year."

In exchange he gave her the ledgers, which no one outside the warden-line had read in two centuries, and watched the finest mind he had ever sat beside walk down his column of sixes with her lips moving. What grew between them across those weeks grew in the only soil either of them had — evidence, shared at lamplight, each the one person alive the other could say the unsayable to. He learned the rest of her situation the way he learned everything that mattered to him: from documents. The letter lying open on her table one evening, the Loranthus line-mother's hand, gracious and inexorable. The family had heard — families always heard — rumours touching her instrument. A voice was a line's banner; a line's purity was read in the strength of its magic; if the banner was to dip, best it dip married, and married well, and soon, while the matching was still hers to make from strength. There were three names in the letter. None of them was a moth-warden's.

"They're not cruel," Sylenne said, watching him read it, because she had left it open to be read; honesty was her one extravagance. "They're old. A line that has held its blood bright for four thousand years doesn't see a person, Theliar. It sees a flame in a procession of lamps, and it sees mine guttering, and it is doing what one does with a guttering lamp: getting it indoors." She took the letter back and folded it with terrible neatness. "And the worst of it — say it for me, warden; you're the one with the rounding rules. If my voice is failing for their reasons — unworthiness, distraction, you — then I'm shamed and they're right. And if it's failing for our reason—"

"Then nothing in Twiland is failing for their reasons," Theliar said. "And nobody's lamp is guttering. The hall is going dark."

The control group, like most of the great instruments of history, was an accident with a clerk attached.

It was the loft's most ordinary business: gift-moths, sent out under seal to friends of Twiland abroad — a betrothal blessing to a half-elven house in Eryndor, a merchant-compact's anniversary courtesy to Timbercross, the small silk diplomacy the Lords had practised for centuries. The office logged the releases; the recipients, charmed, often wrote back; the letters came to the loft for the rolls. Theliar, deep in his column of sixes, was filing one such letter when its pleasantry went through him like cold water: *…and your moth sang to us each evening for a full seven days, to the wonder of the children…*

A full seven. In Verdure. Two hundred leagues from the Tree.

He went through the gift-rolls of three years in one sitting, cross-matching releases against the thank-letters with their artless, priceless testimony — *seven days; a week entire; faded on the eighth morning* — and there it stood, checkable, repeatable, mad. The moths that left the nexus lived their full crowned lives. The moths that stayed home died early. One stock, one loft, one season; the only variable was where the imprint did its living. At the strongest Aetheric ground in the world, the imprint starved. On Verdure's ordinary earth, it fed full.

He brought it to Sylenne at lamplight with his hands not quite steady, and she read the rolls twice, and sat very still, and then said the thing he could not, because her mind ran ahead of his on every road but one: "It's not a fog over everything, then. It's a tide going out. And the Tree—" she laid her hand flat on the table, on the ledgers, on the whole gathering shape of it, "—the Tree is the highest shore in the world, warden. Your loft and my throat aren't where the sickness is worst. They're where the measure is finest. We're not dying first. We're reading first."

They sat with that until the lamps wanted oil. Outside the window the Tree shed its slow silver over the roofs of Luneberg, and neither of them said the other thing, though both now saw it and could not unsee: that the glowvine on the far terraces had gone from the songs' "blazing" to a beautiful, believable soft.

"Marry your three names," Theliar said at last, very quietly, because the evidence was complete now and so was the other thing, and he was a warden, trained from birth to witness a crowned life's exact span without looking away. "If you need to. I'll keep the ledgers either way. But I want it in some record somewhere, once, plainly, before the machinery has us: the finding is ours. Whatever they do with you and whatever becomes of me — the sixes and the frayings are one fact, we found it together, and it is the truest thing I will ever log."

"Write it, then," said Sylenne of Loranthus, and her voice — her failing, priceless, fully sufficient voice — did not fray at all. "Both names. One column."

Aerlys summoned them on the first morning of the Stillness-month preparations, received them alone in the score-room of the Bower, and was, by design, terribly kind.

She had watched long enough. The gossip had the two of them lovers and the voice's failure thereby explained. Her rotation-keepers had the Loranthus family's withdrawal-petition already half-expected. Against all of that, Aerlys had four spring frayings logged in her own ear, a decade of her father's silences, and the duty — hers alone in this city — of judging instruments. So she tested them together, blind, against each other.

"Sing me the third sustaining," she told Sylenne, "as an exercise, at my mark." And to the warden, before the singing: "You brought your brood-frames, as I asked. Choose me a fresh moth, imprint this" — a folded practice-verse, her own hand — "and log what you log."

What happened in the score-room that morning went into no rotation-book, and Aerlys carried it out of the room in three layers.

The outermost layer was the experiment, and it was clean. The voice frayed at the count her gallery log predicted, to within a breath. The moth — imprinted at the same hour, in the same room, on the same ground — took its crown sluggishly, the wing-tremor coming late; the warden, pale, logged the latency unasked and said, "It will return a five, my lady. Perhaps a four. I've seen the waking enough times now to call it." Then the two of them laid out the rest with the humility of people who have checked everything twice — the column of sixes, the failure-map of the rites, the gift-moths' full sevens abroad, the matching of curve to curve, five years deep — and it held. She tried it as her father's daughter, as her singers' judge, and as a Songcaster of two centuries' training, and it held: two instruments of utterly different make, a silk wing and an elven throat, agreeing on the shape of a tide.

The second layer was her own silence. Standing in that room with the proof laid out, Aerlys reached down once more through the morning, down through the Bower's floor to the deep bright current — and felt it full and sweet and unfailing, as she had felt it every day of her life. And understood, at last, what her not-sensing was. Not her father's refutation. His confirmation. Her core was Aethyn-strong, lordline-strong; she drew her water from the deepest part of the well; the lowering had not yet reached her lip.

Ten years she had wanted to sense it, so that her father would not be alone. The finding, when it came, was crueller and kinder than sensing: he had never been alone. The whole shore had been agreeing with him for a decade, in ledgers and frayings, in a language no lord was listening to.

The third layer she gave no name. It was the Concord. Four years away; the song half-composed in the folded notation she carried everywhere; the great gathering of the bloodlines at the Tree, for which a Chief Songcaster writes the song that says what the Tree is. She had been composing, she saw now, a song for a Tree that was no longer entirely the case.

"The finding is sound," she said at last, formally, to the two of them — and watched hope and dread arrive together in both faces, because sound meant believed, and believed meant consequences. "Now. Before I tell you what I will do with it, you will each tell me, plainly: what is it going to cost you?"

It was the kindest question either of them was asked that year, and the last easy hour they had.

The machinery, once engaged, moved with the gentleness of everything elven and the inevitability of everything old, and it took them in opposite directions, exactly as they had chosen.

They had chosen. That must be set down, because everything else was taken from them and the choosing was not. In the score-room, before Aerlys, each had been offered the soft road and declined it in front of the other.

Sylenne could have carried her failure indoors — withdrawn quietly to the southern groves "for rest," voice and reputation intact, the frayings folded away among family, and the failure-map lost with her. She declared it instead: formally, to the Bower, in writing — *the instrument is failing at the deep draws and the cause is in the ground* — and her line's machinery, gracious and inexorable, responded as the letter had promised. The withdrawal-petition became a recall. The three names became one name, a Loranthus-allied house in the southern groves, a match made quickly from what strength remained. She did not fight it. "The family is wrong about the cause and right about the clock," she told Theliar, in the lantern-loft, the last week, with her terrible Loranthus honesty. "My voice has perhaps a decade of rites in it. I'd spend it here. They'll spend it on the line. It was never going to be mine to spend, warden — that was the dream part. The finding was the true part. We got the true part into the rolls."

And Theliar could have softened the ledger. It was put to him, with perfect courtesy, by the loft's own elders — not falsify; merely annotate: weak stock suspected, recount pending, the sixes held in abeyance, the office's standing protected, the alarming column kept decently provisional for a decade or three. He wrote instead the fair copy of everything: the sixes, the four, the latency-log from the score-room, the gift-moth sevens with their cross-matched letters; and at the head of it, against every convention of an office that had been anonymous for eleven generations, the finding with both names upon it — *S. Loranthus, Canopy Singer; T. Velwyn, moth-warden of the Tree.* One column, as promised. The elders received it in silence. The silence had generations in it.

It went up from the loft on the last morning of the year, by the channel such things had always theoretically had and never once used: warden to office, office to the Bower, the Bower's Chief Songcaster carrying it personally — Aerlys had insisted; it was the one mercy wholly in her gift — up through the living halls to the study of the Lord of Luneberg. Theliar was not present for the handing-over. He heard of it as the office heard: that the Lord Caelindor had received the ledgers of the moth-wardens into his keeping. That he had asked the Chief Songcaster three questions, and then no more. And that he had stood afterward a long time at the study's window, where the whole breadth of the Tree fills the glass, holding the fair copy the way one holds a thing long expected and never wanted.

The proof. The one measurable proof. It has not left his study since.

Her father said nothing on the evening walk, and Aerlys, walking beside him under the waymarkers in the Tree's slow light, finally understood the silence she had spent ten years trying to relieve, because she was inside it now herself.

She had carried the ledgers up to him expecting — she examined it honestly, afterward — some unburdening: the decade's weight shared at last, father and daughter on the same side of the knowledge. What she met instead was the look she had seen once before in her life, at her mother's burying: the look of a man receiving a thing he must now protect her from, taking the weight of her own finding back out of her hands the way one takes a blade from a child — gently, by the flat. He asked his three questions: provenance, witnesses, copies. He thanked her with entire love. And he closed the study door; and on the walk that evening he spoke of the autumn, and of her mother's roses, and of nothing; and Aerlys matched his pace and his silence and did not press, because she had finally learned what the silence was made of. Not distrust. Sequence. He was deciding something larger than Luneberg — she could feel the size of it beside her in the dark — and a thing that size, half-told, would be a rumour with a lord's name on it. So she walked on, trusted and not yet fully informed, and let him hold what he held, and held her own.

Hers was the song.

She began it again that winter — struck through four years of notation in one evening and began again — and what came, slowly, in the new year, was music the Bower had no category for: the Concord song of 1150 recast in what her working notes called the lowered mode. The old Concord songs were proclamations: the Tree blazing, the lines eternal, the current without floor. She could not write that song now; she had stood in a score-room and watched the floor be measured. So she wrote what was true instead, in the only language she had that could carry truth past doctrine: a song built around a long third sustaining that deliberately does not rise to the old height — that holds, instead, lower, longer, steadier, and makes of the lower holding something so beautiful that no bloodline at the Concord would be able to call it loss until they had already wept at it. A song for a diminished Tree, that taught the diminishment as a kind of evening. *Let them learn it the way the shore learns the tide*, she wrote in the notation's margin. *Singing it. By heart. Before anyone says the word.*

The warden she kept. Quietly, against the murmurs of the office's elders, on her own authority: the loft's fair copies came now to the Bower as well, and once a season the young man with the careful hands sat in her score-room and walked her down the year's columns, and they did not speak of the southern groves. The one time a gift-moth arrived for him there — it sang sixteen rising phrases of the Lifting of the Leaves in a voice like water in sun, flawless, every fraying mastered or mended for the length of one imprinted message, and faded on the sill on the morning of the seventh day, having lived, so far from the Tree had it flown, its whole crowned span — Aerlys left the loft before the third phrase, because some columns are not hers to read.

Three things the wind cannot carry, the old Singers said: a kept oath, a true love, and a song already sung. The moth-light over Luneberg dims by such degrees that only ledgers see it. The Concord comes in 1150. The song is nearly ready.
