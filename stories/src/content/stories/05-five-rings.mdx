The first thing the girl from Verdure did when she arrived at the ruins of Aureon was draw a building nobody had asked her to draw.

It was not a reproduction of what was there — the ruins were everywhere, patient in their rubble, and any of the surveyors already on site could have drawn a ruin. It was a building that would fit on the flat ground between the broken grain stores and the old Lantern, a two-storey structure with a specific pitch of roof and a particular attitude toward its neighbouring walls: not crowding them, not deferring to them, just present beside them in the way that suited the site. She drew it in twenty minutes standing at the edge of the site on her first morning, and when the surveyor's foreman came to tell her where she was supposed to be, she showed it to him automatically, without thinking, the way she did everything that came from the drawing hand.

He stared at it for a long moment. "Who asked for that?"

"Nobody," said Mara Vonne. "It just — belongs there. Don't you think?"

The foreman had been surveying ruins for six years and had never been asked whether a building belonged there. He told her where she was supposed to be, which was the archive wing's western extent, measuring void spaces for the inventory. She went to the archive wing and measured void spaces and drew three more buildings before the noon watch, none of which had been asked for and all of which, she was increasingly sure, belonged somewhere in the emerging idea of what this ruined city could become.

The dwarf had come south for reasons he described, when asked, as administrative and found, when not asked, to be something more like homesickness for a kind of noise he could not produce in the mountain.

Torvin Grasp — this was not his mountain name but the one he gave at the gates because the gate-clerks and the mountain-name had an adversarial history — had held the office of yield-surveyor for the Grimgar compact's southern interests for twelve years, which meant he spent those years in argument with human factors over grain-weights and road-quality and whether a certain kind of Verdure timber met the spec the mountain required. It was not a post that asked for the quality he was best at, which was making things work in proximity that had previously refused to; the compact's southern interests were handled with a kind of efficiency that left no room for the more interesting category of problem.

What was happening at Aureon, in the camp that had begun to gather around the site in the two years since the empire's formal end, was the most interesting category of problem Torvin had ever heard of described. Something was being made. It was not clear yet what, or by whom, or on what authority — the last question being the interesting one, because the answer was clearly something like by everyone, and not clearly yet what kind of instrument that was. He had asked to be seconded from the compact's southern office for a year's consultancy on the mountain's interests in the new arrangement, framed the request in the most administrative language available, and arrived in the camp two days after the girl from Verdure.

He found her at the edge of the former grain-district at the second watch, drawing a building nobody had asked her to draw.

"What is that?" he asked, because the drawing was very good and the question seemed required.

"Where the new granary should go," Mara said. "If you put it here, it opens the approach to the archive wing. The other proposals block it."

"I've read the other proposals. They're awful." Torvin studied the drawing. "The Lantern's alignment is wrong here, though. The light hits the south face and you'll cook grain in summer."

She looked at it. Then she looked at him. "How did you know that?"

"I've been reading about this site for six months. The Lantern's orientation is in the survey notes. The light issue is in the fifth one." He paused. "The granary should rotate twenty degrees east. Then the south face is in the archive wing's shadow from the third watch onward."

Mara picked up her pen and rotated the granary twenty degrees east in the drawing, and they both looked at it.

"Yes," she said.

"Yes," he said.

They went to find whoever was currently deciding things, which was a more complicated question at this site than at most, and which turned out to be the answer to a problem they had not known was a problem yet.

The council that had gathered in the old council chamber — cold, partially re-roofed, its east wall open to the courtyard in a way that had been temporary for four months — represented seven different interests, six different cities, and one compact, and its main achievement in its first year of operation had been to table the question of what it was.

This was not inaction. It was the most difficult thing the council was asked to do, because every party present knew what kind of instrument it wanted to be and was aware that announcing this would cause the other parties to leave. Torvin, who had been sent to observe and report back to the compact, attended his first full session and found it recognizable as a specific kind of problem: the initial stage of an agreement between parties who want different things and need each other enough to keep sitting in the same room.

He had built four such agreements in his twelve years on the southern circuit. Three had held. The method that worked, and the reason the compact paid for the consultancy, was not clever in the way that clever methods are clever — it was patient and specific and dull and could only be done by a person who was willing to make everyone equally dissatisfied in the short term, which was a quality most people found, in themselves, that they did not possess at the decisive moment.

Mara was not sent to the council sessions. She was drawing buildings. But the buildings — which had been accumulating for two months on the site's central notice-board without anyone formally authorising them, because they were so obviously right for the sites they proposed that no party wanted to be the one to object — were doing something to the council that the council was not fully aware of. They were giving it something to agree about that wasn't the council's own nature.

If the granary goes here, this is what the street between the granary and the archive becomes. If the archive wing opens at this face rather than the other, this is the public space that results. The decisions about buildings were not constitutional decisions. They were material decisions, concrete, involving specific quantities of stone and specific sight-lines and the question of which children would walk which way to school. The parties who could not agree on the council's nature could agree about where grain was stored, if the agreement was arrived at in the right order.

Torvin suggested, at the sixth session, that the council constitute itself provisionally as a coordinating body for the building programme. Not a governing body. A coordinating body. The difference was a word, and the word was everything. Governing required deciding who governed. Coordinating required only that things be coordinated.

Three parties objected. Four, with varying degrees of reluctance, agreed to try it.

Torvin wrote in his report to the compact: *The agreement is provisional and structurally fragile. It will hold long enough to build the first ring. If the first ring goes up correctly, it will hold long enough to design the second. The city is the argument in its best form.*

<Break />

The five rings were not called that during the building. They were called *phases* during the building, which was less poetic and more accurate, and the fifth was not completed until nine years after Torvin returned to the mountain and twenty-two years after Mara drew the first building. But the form was established in the first two years, in the agreement that Torvin brokered and the buildings Mara designed, and the form held.

The first ring went up in the year 927. It contained the new granary (rotated twenty degrees east), the archive wing's primary public face, the Sunward Gate with its rebuilt approach, and forty-three residential structures along the new Greenrun road alignment. Mara had drawn all of them, and the ones that were built were the ones the council had agreed on through the coordinating body's new process, which was slow and contentious and produced, each time, a result that four of seven parties could live with, which was the best any process of its kind could do and was, in practice, enough.

On the day the first ring's last gate was hung, the council held a session in the open air for the first time, in the public space that had resulted from the archive wing's orientation, and declared itself — with the kind of exhausted decision that bodies make when they have been a thing long enough to admit it — a permanent institution. Not a governing council. A city council. The difference was specific and had to do with what kind of authority the five-ring agreement had created, and Torvin, reading the account in the compact's records eighteen months later, thought: *there it is*.

Mara stayed. She drew buildings for the next forty years, and the city that was made from her drawings is the Everpeak that people inhabit now, which is not the city she drew but is descended from the drawings in the way that cities descend from the agreements that made them. The first building she drew — the granary with the rotated face, the light falling on stone in the way the fifth survey note predicted — was demolished in the third ring to make way for a thoroughfare, which she had also designed, and which is still there.

She did not complain about the granary. She had drawn it in twenty minutes on her first morning, and it had held the shape of the city long enough to be replaced by the city it made possible. She thought this was how buildings should work, and said so, once, to a student who asked about the demolition. The student wrote it down. It is in the archive, under her name, filed where things are kept that were said once and need to be found again.
